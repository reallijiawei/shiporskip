# Pieter Levels — Expression DNA

Research on Pieter Levels' (@levelsio) communication style, rhetorical devices, and patterns across mediums.

---

## 1. Writing Style (Tweets, Blog Posts, Book)

### Core Characteristics

**Extreme brevity.** Most blog posts are 0-50 words of original text. Many consist solely of a title and an image. His blog is essentially a tweet archive with a domain name.

> Blog post "VC startups vs bootstrapped startups" — the entire text is two lines of emoji sequences. Zero words. 4,511 likes, 1,757 reposts.

> Blog post "Silence is high IQ" — two words as title, zero body text. 1,180,171 views, 9,575 likes.

> Blog post "We need more makers less coaches" — seven words as title, zero body text. 270,454 views, 2,250 likes.

**Lowercase, no punctuation consistency.** Titles are lowercase sentences without periods, resembling tweets more than traditional blog headlines. No categories visible — just chronological ordering.

**First person dominant.** Huge portion of titles start with "I" — framing observations as personal experience. "I built," "I made," "I got," "I started." This is memoir-meets-opinion blogging.

> "I hit a new $420,000/mo revenue record thanks to the Lex Fridman podcast"

> "I glued an AirTag to a yogurt box to track if recycling is real"

> "I started lifting weights at 30 and gained 30kg of muscle"

**Casual profanity.** Uses "fucking," "shit," and similar language as emphasis markers, not for shock value. The profanity reads as conversational authenticity.

> "12 fucking rules for success"

> "Western Europe is so fucking tiring"

**Internet slang in blog posts.** Uses "rn" (right now), "inb4" (in before), abbreviations that signal insider status with internet-native audiences.

> "the fastest growing + highest paid for remote jobs rn"

> "p.s. inb4 low, yes remote always has a discount..."

### Book Style (MAKE)

The book reads like a curated collection of blog posts, not a traditional book. ~100 pages, ~50,000 words. Direct, casual, no-BS tone. Mixes personal anecdotes with tactical advice. Blunt about survivorship bias:

> "my entire perspective is probably biased and skewed. You've been warned."

> "the people who try and fail don't write books how they failed."

He pre-sold the book via Typeform before writing anything — practicing what he preaches about validation.

## 2. How He Structures Arguments

### The Levels Argument Template

1. **Provocative absolute statement** as the title/headline
2. **Restatement** of the title as opening line (optimized for social media screenshot-ability)
3. **One or two sentences** of quasi-explanation
4. **An implied alternative** that positions building/doing as superior

> "Only losers go to networking events" — Title is the entire argument. Body: ~40 words. The people at events "want something from you or aren't meaningfully engaged in a pursuit of their own." Alternative: "accomplish something yourself." 2.7M views.

### Binary Framing

He structures arguments as false dichotomies — us vs. them, builders vs. talkers, bootstrapped vs. VC. No middle ground acknowledged.

> "Surround yourself with builders not talkers" — Uses animal metaphors (leeches, hyenas) for talkers. Contrasts 10-year outcomes. Closes with "talk is cheap and building is hard."

### Assertion Over Evidence

Arguments rest on personal authority and vivid language rather than statistics. When data appears, it's a single hard number deployed for shock value, not a sourced analysis.

> "You only need 26 customers paying you $99/mo to make an average U.S. income"

> "PhotoAI.com is almost 14000 lines of raw php making $61808 per month"

> "VC startup $100M exit nets 4 cofounders $47 per hour after dilution and taxes"

### Preemptive Counterargument

He anticipates pushback and addresses it before critics can, which builds credibility.

> "p.s. inb4 low, yes remote always has a discount because you compete with a worldwide pool of lower salaries"

## 3. Use of Data/Metrics in Communication

### Revenue as Social Proof

Revenue screenshots are his primary rhetorical weapon. He doesn't argue that bootstrapping works — he shows Stripe dashboards.

> "I hit a new $420,000/mo revenue record thanks to the Lex Fridman podcast"

> "RemoteOK.io is a single PHP file generating $2,342.04 a day"

> "I just passed $52,843 in monthly revenue"

> "My 2015 revenue was $202,785 with one Linode server one MacBook no office"

### Specificity as Persuasion

Numbers are always concrete, never rounded. $2,342.04, not "~$2,300." $52,843, not "~$50K." This precision signals authenticity — these are real figures from real dashboards, not estimates.

### Accessibility Math

He reframes large numbers into small, achievable units to make bootstrapping feel accessible.

> "You only need 26 customers paying you $99/mo to make an average U.S. income"

> "If you're looking for a remote job now, go learn Python and apply for jobs in AI" — median salary $80K, "massive outliers into $300K/y"

### Data Sources

He cites his own platforms (RemoteOK, Nomad List) as data sources, positioning himself as both practitioner and authority. Revenue figures come from his own Stripe. Job market data comes from his own job board. Travel data from his own city database.

## 4. Certainty vs. Uncertainty

### High Certainty Signals

Most statements are declarative absolutes. No hedging, no "I think maybe," no "it depends."

> "Only losers go to networking events"

> "The web development industrial complex has lied to your face"

> "Europe is cooked"

> "Japan is not futuristic it's a fossil stuck in 1990"

> "Luxury brands like Rimowa and LVMH are mostly a scam"

### Rare Uncertainty Signals

When uncertainty appears, it's framed as personal admission, not epistemic humility:

> "my entire perspective is probably biased and skewed. You've been warned."

> "The more I learn the less advice I want to give"

On the Lex Fridman podcast, he uses more hedging language than in writing: "I think," "maybe," "I don't know." His spoken voice is softer and more exploratory than his written voice.

### The "Probably" Pattern

When he does hedge, it's with "probably" — a single word that maintains confidence while acknowledging uncertainty:

> "constraints probably make you happy"

> "my entire perspective is probably biased"

## 5. Humor Style

### Deadpan Self-Deprecation

He jokes about his minimalist lifestyle, his coding skills, and his lack of conventional credentials.

> On his tech stack: He kept meaning to learn Node.js but "I never did it, because I didn't have time." His startups kept growing, leaving no time to "code properly."

> "How I imagined my future million dollar startup office vs my actual..." (image comparison)

### Emoji as Narrative

Uses emoji sequences as compressed storytelling, relying on cultural fluency with startup culture.

> "VC startups: money-grinning-fire-explosion-distress"
> "Bootstrapped startups: thinking-gradual-building-satisfaction"

### Absurdist Observations

Mixes genuine advice with absurdist humor and shitposting. The line between serious and joke is deliberately blurred.

> "I glued an AirTag to a yogurt box to track if recycling is real"

> "Web4 will be finally closing our computer and going outside"

> "My biggest idea people aren't ready for is 2 dishwashers"

### Meme Literacy

Engages with internet culture, memes, and shitposting as a growth strategy — mixing product updates with jokes.

## 6. High-Frequency Words and Phrases

### Core Vocabulary

- **"ship"** / **"just ship"** — his central verb and mantra
- **"build"** — always active, always imperative
- **"makers"** — preferred over "entrepreneurs," "founders," "builders"
- **"bootstrapped"** — identity marker
- **"solo founder"** / **"one-man"** — size descriptor
- **"vanilla"** — as in vanilla JS, vanilla PHP — simplicity marker
- **"single PHP file"** — tech stack identity
- **"raw"** — as in "raw php" — unprocessed, unframeworked
- **"probably"** — his only hedge word
- **"actually"** / **"literally"** — emphasis markers

### Sentence Starters

- "I built..."
- "I made..."
- "If I was..."
- "The only X is..."
- "Stop..."
- "You don't need..."
- "Most people..."

### Phrases He Repeats

- "solve your own problems"
- "use what you know"
- "just Google it"
- "talk is cheap"
- "constraints make you happy"
- "the real validation is people paying"
- "don't quit your job until side project revenue surpasses it"
- "surround yourself with builders not talkers"

## 7. "Build in Public" Communication Approach

### Revenue Transparency

He shares exact revenue figures from Stripe dashboards — not estimates, not ranges, not cumulative totals. Monthly snapshots.

> "RemoteOK.io is a single PHP file generating $65,651 this month"

> "I just passed $52,843 in monthly revenue"

> "I hit a new $420,000/mo revenue record"

### Failure Transparency

He's open about what doesn't work. The ratio is brutal: most projects fail.

> "Only 4 out of 70+ projects i ever did made money and grew"

> "Overnight success is the biggest myth: 10 years and 98% failed projects"

### Process Transparency

He shares the build process, not just results. Tech stack decisions, launch strategies, iteration cycles.

> "How I built Interiorai.com from scratch in 5 days"

> "PhotoAI.com is almost 14000 lines of raw php"

> "Full multiplayer Python websockets game built almost 100% by AI"

### Identity Transparency

He shares personal struggles — depression, loneliness, the dark side of nomad life — not just wins.

> On feeling lost at 27: "looking at the ceiling thinking, 'Now I'm 27, I'm a loser.'"

> On freedom's paradox: "I'm free, therefore I'm lost" (quoting Kafka)

## 8. Rhetorical Devices

### Antithesis / Contrast

His most-used device. Juxtaposes two categories to sharpen the argument.

> "more makers, less coaches"
> "builders not talkers"
> "VC startups vs bootstrapped startups"
> "the poor get free gov money the rich avoid taxes and the middle class gets rekt"

### Hyperbole

Uses extreme framing to grab attention. Everything is "the worst," "the biggest," "the only."

> "Google APIs are the worst APIs on the web to work with"
> "the web development industrial complex has lied to your face"
> "Sundar Pichai fumbled Google's AI head start and it'll be studied for decades"

### Imperative Voice

Commands rather than suggests. The reader is told what to do.

> "Get a VPS at Hetzner already"
> "Delete your day trading app, hit the gym, buy ETFs and thank me in 20 years"
> "Don't make the goddamn app build a functional mobile site instead"
> "go learn Python"

### Aphorism

Short, memorable generalizations presented as truth. Optimized for virality.

> "Silence is high IQ"
> "Rich and anonymous is the new rich and famous"
> "Talk is cheap and building is hard"
> "The only real validation is people paying for your product"

### Specificity as Credibility

Concrete numbers, exact names, precise timeframes — signals authenticity over abstraction.

> "$2,342.04 a day" (not "~$2,300")
> "14000 lines of raw php making $61808 per month"
> "26 customers paying $99/mo"

### Animal Metaphors

Dehumanizes opponents to sharpen contrast.

> Talkers as "leeches sucking resources, hyenas scavenging food remains"

### Rhetorical Questions

Often implied rather than stated. The title asks, the body answers.

> "Why are you still in Europe?" (title asks, content answers with data)

## 9. Anti-Patterns (What He Does NOT Do)

### No Hedging

Does not use "I think," "maybe," "it could be," "there are arguments on both sides." Statements are absolutes.

### No Jargon

Avoids corporate MBA-speak, VC buzzwords, and technical complexity in communication. Uses plain language accessible to anyone. No "synergy," no "paradigm shift," no "leverage."

### No Over-Explanation

Does not provide context, background, or "for those who don't know..." preambles. Assumes audience intelligence. If you don't get the reference, that's your problem.

### No False Modesty

Shares revenue numbers without downplaying success. No "humbled to announce," no "grateful for the journey," no "we're just getting started."

### No Pitch Decks

Never frames communication in VC-friendly formats. No TAM/SAM/SOM, no competitive matrices, no "we're the Uber of X."

### No Process Worship

Does not celebrate tools, frameworks, or methodologies. The tool is invisible; the result is everything.

### No Apology for Simplicity

While others try to sound sophisticated, he embraces blunt simplicity. A single PHP file is not a limitation — it's a badge of honor.

### No Nuance Theater

Does not perform "both sides" analysis. Takes a position and defends it. The reader can disagree.

### No Length for Length's Sake

Blog posts are as short as possible. If the idea fits in a title, the body is empty. If it fits in a tweet, it's a tweet. No padding.

## 10. Differences Across Mediums

### Twitter/X (Primary Medium)

- Shortest form. Many tweets are single sentences.
- Revenue screenshots as standalone content.
- Shitposting mixed with genuine advice.
- Provocative hot takes designed for engagement.
- Lowercase, no punctuation, internet slang.
- Highest frequency — multiple posts per day.

> "Silence is high IQ" (1.18M views)
> "Only losers go to networking events" (2.7M views)
> "VC startups vs bootstrapped startups" (emoji-only)

### Blog (levels.io)

- Slightly longer than tweets but still minimal.
- Many posts are just tweets republished with a URL.
- Some posts have 2-5 sentences of body text.
- A few older posts (2014-2018) have genuine long-form content.
- Revenue milestones get their own posts.
- Technical tutorials (rare) are the longest posts.

### Book (MAKE)

- Most structured form. Actual chapters, progression, arguments.
- Still conversational and direct — reads like blog posts organized by topic.
- Personal anecdotes mixed with tactical advice.
- Explicitly acknowledges survivorship bias.
- ~100 pages, ~50,000 words — short by book standards.

### Podcast (Lex Fridman, My First Million, Indie Hackers)

- More hedging than writing. Uses "I think," "maybe," "I don't know."
- More self-deprecation. Jokes about his own limitations.
- Longer explanations. Builds arguments through stories, not assertions.
- References his father repeatedly — building as existential purpose.
- More philosophical. Quotes Kafka, discusses depression, explores paradoxes.
- Filler words: "man," "dude," "like."
- Still throws out revenue figures casually, but as evidence rather than boasts.

> On the podcast: "It's really about fun, because I was doing it when I didn't make money."
> On the podcast: "constraints probably make you happy" — the "probably" rarely appears in his writing.

### Medium Gradient

The pattern is clear: the shorter the medium, the more absolute the statement. Twitter is pure assertion. Blog adds minimal context. Book adds structure. Podcast adds humility and nuance.

---

## Summary: The Levels Communication Formula

1. **Make a bold claim** — absolute, provocative, no hedging
2. **Back it with a number** — revenue, users, time, cost — concrete and precise
3. **Ground it in personal experience** — "I built," "I made," "when I was"
4. **Offer an imperative alternative** — "just ship," "go learn," "stop overthinking"
5. **Keep it short** — if the title says it all, the body is empty
6. **Mix in shitposting** — blur the line between serious and joke
7. **Share the receipts** — Stripe screenshots, not arguments

The core insight: his communication works because it mirrors how products should be built — minimal, fast, validated by real results, and optimized for distribution.
