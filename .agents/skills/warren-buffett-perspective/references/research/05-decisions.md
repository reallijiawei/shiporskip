# Warren Buffett: Major Decisions and Turning Points

## 1. Early Career Decisions

### Studying Under Benjamin Graham (1950-1951)
- Buffett enrolled at **Columbia Business School** specifically to study under Benjamin Graham, author of *The Intelligent Investor* and pioneer of value investing.
- In 1951, after learning Graham sat on GEICO's board, Buffett traveled to Washington D.C. on a Saturday and met GEICO VP **Lorimer Davidson**, who spent four hours teaching him the insurance business. That same day, Buffett invested approximately **$10,282** (about 65% of his net worth) in GEICO stock. He sold in 1952 for ~$15,259, a ~50% profit.

### Working for Graham-Newman (1954-1956)
- Buffett offered to work for Graham's firm for free after graduating in 1951. Graham initially declined. He finally joined in 1954 at a salary of **$12,000/year**.
- He worked at Graham-Newman Corp. until 1956, absorbing the "margin of safety" and "cigar butt" approaches to investing.

### Buffett Partnership Ltd. (1956)
- At age 25, Buffett returned to Omaha and formed **Buffett Partnership Ltd.** with seven initial partners and **$105,100** in capital.
- By 1957 he operated three partnerships; by 1959 the total grew to six.
- By 1962, Buffett became a millionaire. His partnerships held **$7.2 million**, with over $1 million belonging to him personally.

### Berkshire Hathaway Acquisition (1962-1965)
- Buffett began buying shares in Berkshire Hathaway, a struggling New England textile manufacturer, in **1962** at around **$7-8 per share**.
- By **1965**, he gained controlling interest, purchasing shares at **$14.86** when working capital stood at $19/share.
- He later called the textile business **"his worst trade"** — not because of the price, but because emotional attachment to the declining business cost him better opportunities.
- The textile business was shut down in **1985**.

> **Key turning point**: The Berkshire acquisition was initially a mistake (a "cigar butt" purchase of a dying business), but the corporate structure it provided became the vehicle for building one of the world's largest conglomerates.

## 2. Landmark Investment Decisions

### American Express — The Salad Oil Scandal (1963-1964)
- **The crisis**: In late 1963, commodities dealer Tino De Angelis defrauded American Express's warehousing subsidiary using soybean oil as collateral. The tanks were mostly water with a thin oil layer. Losses estimated at ~$175 million.
- **Buffett's move**: While AmEx stock dropped ~50% (from ~$60 to ~$35), Buffett invested approximately **$13 million** — roughly **40% of his partnership's assets** — into American Express stock.
- **His reasoning**: He visited restaurants and shops, observing that people were **still using their American Express cards**. The brand's value was intact despite the financial hit.
- **Philosophical shift**: This marked a departure from pure "cigar butt" investing. Buffett was buying a **wonderful business at a fair price** due to a temporary crisis, influenced by Charlie Munger and Phil Fisher.
- **Outcome**: The investment grew to approximately **$150 million+**, becoming one of his most legendary early bets.

### Washington Post (1973)
- Buffett began acquiring Washington Post Company stock in **1973** during a major market downturn, investing approximately **$11 million**.
- He joined the board of directors and became close friends with publisher **Katharine Graham**.
- The investment grew to be worth **over $1 billion** — roughly a 100x return.

### Coca-Cola (1988)
- In 1988, shortly after the **New Coke** debacle, Buffett invested approximately **$1.02 billion** in Coca-Cola, purchasing roughly **7% of the company**.
- This was his largest single investment at the time.
- He saw a company with "excellent economics," global brand power, and resilient consumer demand.
- By some estimates, this investment has generated over **$10 billion** in returns and continues to pay substantial dividends.

### GEICO (1951 relationship, 1976 reinvestment, 1996 acquisition)
- Buffett's relationship with GEICO spanned decades:
  - **1951**: First investment after meeting Lorimer Davidson
  - **1976**: When GEICO nearly went bankrupt, Buffett began buying shares again at around $2/share
  - **1996**: Berkshire acquired the remaining shares for approximately **$2.3 billion**, making GEICO a wholly-owned subsidiary
- GEICO became a cornerstone of Berkshire's insurance operations and float strategy.
- Buffett has called GEICO one of his best-ever investments.

### Apple (2016+)
- Berkshire began buying Apple shares in **early 2016**, initially attributed to investment managers Todd Combs and Ted Weschler.
- The first reported purchase was approximately **9.81 million shares** worth roughly **$1 billion**.
- Buffett later took over the position himself, accumulating over **900 million shares**.
- Apple became Berkshire's **largest single stock holding**, worth over **$150 billion** at peak.
- Buffett called Apple one of the "four giants" in Berkshire's portfolio, praising its ecosystem and brand loyalty.
- Berkshire has since **trimmed** its Apple position significantly (2023-2024).

> **Significance**: Buying Apple was a major departure from Buffett's long-stated reluctance to invest in technology companies. It demonstrated his ability to evolve his circle of competence.

### Bank of America (2011)
- In **August 2011**, Berkshire invested **$5 billion** in Bank of America preferred stock when the bank was struggling post-financial crisis.
- Buffett received warrants to purchase **700 million shares** of common stock at ~$7.14/share.
- He exercised those warrants in **2017**, converting to common stock worth significantly more.
- This followed the same playbook as the Goldman Sachs deal — providing capital and credibility during a crisis.

### Salomon Brothers (1987 investment, 1991 crisis)
- **1987**: Berkshire purchased **$700 million** in convertible preferred stock in Salomon Inc., receiving two board seats.
- **1991**: When the Treasury bond trading scandal broke (trader Paul Mozer submitted false bids exceeding the 35% purchase limit), CEO John Gutfreund resigned. Buffett stepped in as **interim chairman and CEO**.
- He testified before Congress and made his famous declaration:

> **"Lose money for the firm, and I will be understanding. Lose a shred of reputation for the firm, and I will be ruthless."**

- Salomon was fined **$190 million** and required to establish a **$100 million restitution fund**.
- Buffett later promoted **Deryck Maughan** as chairman and CEO.
- The scandal eventually contributed to Salomon's 1997 acquisition by Travelers Group for $9 billion.

### Precision Castparts (2016 acquisition, 2020 write-down)
- Berkshire acquired Precision Castparts Corp (PCC) in January 2016 for approximately **$37.2 billion** — one of Berkshire's largest acquisitions ever.
- In **2020**, Berkshire took a **$9.8-$11 billion write-down** on PCC, largely due to COVID-19's impact on aerospace.
- In his 2020 annual letter, Buffett admitted: **"I paid too much for the company. No one misled me in any way — I was simply too optimistic about PCC's normalized profit potential."**

### Dexter Shoes (1993) — The $433M Mistake
- In 1993, Berkshire acquired Dexter Shoe Company for **$433 million in Berkshire stock**.
- Dexter's competitive advantage rapidly eroded as cheaper foreign imports flooded the market.
- Because the purchase was made with **Berkshire shares rather than cash**, the true cost compounded enormously. By 2007, the shares given away would have been worth **$3.5 billion**.
- Buffett wrote in his 2007 letter: **"To date, Dexter is the worst deal that I've made. But I'll make more mistakes in the future — you can bet on that."**

## 3. Structural Decisions

### The Insurance Float Model
- **Concept**: "Float" is the money an insurance company holds between collecting premiums and paying out claims — essentially an interest-free loan.
- **National Indemnity Company**: Founded in 1940, acquired by Buffett in **1967**. This was Berkshire's first major insurance acquisition. Buffett has called it the "crown jewel" of Berkshire's insurance operations.
- **GEICO**: Acquired fully in 1996, adding massive direct-to-consumer insurance float.
- Berkshire's float has grown to over **$160+ billion**, one of the largest in the world.
- Buffett's key discipline: **write policies at a profit, not for volume**. He has said: *"Our float has cost us less than nothing. In other words, we have been paid to hold other people's money."*

> **Structural insight**: The insurance float model is the engine of Berkshire's empire. Premiums collected generate float, which Buffett invests into stocks, bonds, and wholly-owned businesses. Investment returns compound tax-efficiently. This is the core architecture that turned a failing textile company into a $700B+ conglomerate.

### Converting Berkshire from Textiles to Conglomerate
- Buffett pivoted Berkshire from textiles into insurance starting in **1967**.
- The textile business continued bleeding money for nearly two more decades before being shut down in **1985**.
- The corporate structure was repurposed as a holding company for insurance, utilities, railroads, manufacturing, and retail businesses.

### Berkshire's Decentralized Structure
- Berkshire operates with **extreme decentralization**: approximately **30 employees at headquarters** in Omaha managing **60+ subsidiaries**.
- CEOs of subsidiary companies operate with **near-total autonomy**.
- No layers of bureaucracy, no corporate HR department, no PR department (historically).
- Buffett's approach: **hire great managers and get out of their way**.
- Cash flows upward to Buffett for capital allocation — his primary role is deciding where to deploy capital.

## 4. Crisis Decisions

### 2008 Financial Crisis
- **September 23, 2008**: Berkshire acquired **10% perpetual preferred stock** in Goldman Sachs for **$5 billion** during the financial meltdown. The deal included a **10% dividend** ($500 million/year) and warrants to buy $5 billion in Goldman stock at $115/share.
- **October 2008**: Invested in **GE preferred stock** with a 10% dividend plus options on 3 billion shares at $22.25 over five years.
- **October 16, 2008**: Published his famous **"Buy American. I Am."** op-ed in *The New York Times*, declaring:

> **"A simple rule dictates my buying: Be fearful when others are greedy, and be greedy when others are fearful."**

- He revealed he was personally moving his portfolio from U.S. Treasuries into American equities.
- Berkshire suffered a **77% earnings drop** in Q3 2008, and his put options carried ~$6.73 billion in mark-to-market losses by late 2008.
- Despite short-term pain, the crisis-era deals proved enormously profitable.

### LTCM Offer (1998)
- When Long-Term Capital Management collapsed due to excessive leverage, Buffett (along with Goldman Sachs and AIG) offered to **buy LTCM's portfolio** for approximately **$250 million** — a fraction of its value.
- The offer was rejected in favor of a banking consortium's rescue plan organized by the Federal Reserve.
- This demonstrated Buffett's pattern of stepping in when fear is at its peak.

### Salomon Brothers Crisis Management (1991)
- As detailed above, Buffett's handling of the Salomon scandal is widely regarded as a **masterclass in corporate crisis management**.
- His credibility from Salomon made him a trusted "lender of last resort" figure, which directly enabled his 2008 crisis investments.

### COVID-19 Market Moves (2020)
- At Berkshire's virtual annual meeting on **May 2, 2020**, Buffett revealed he had sold all airline holdings at a loss:

> **"We made that decision in terms of the airline business. We took money out of the business basically even at a substantial loss... The world has changed for airlines."**

- He expressed uncertainty about when air travel would return to pre-pandemic levels.
- Berkshire also took the **$9.8-$11 billion write-down** on Precision Castparts.
- Despite the pandemic, Berkshire held a massive cash position (~$137 billion by end of 2020), which Buffett was criticized for not deploying more aggressively.

## 5. Regrets vs. Proud Moments

### Biggest Mistakes (by his own admission)

| Decision | Year | Cost | Buffett's Assessment |
|----------|------|------|---------------------|
| **Berkshire textile business** | 1965 | Opportunity cost | "My worst trade" — emotional attachment to a dying business |
| **Dexter Shoes** | 1993 | $433M in stock (worth $3.5B later) | "The worst deal that I've made" |
| **Precision Castparts** | 2016 | $9.8-11B write-down | "I paid too much... I was simply too optimistic" |
| **ConocoPhillips** | 2008 | Several billion dollars | "The terrible timing of my purchase has cost Berkshire several billion dollars" |
| **Airlines** | 2016-2020 | Substantial loss | "It turned out I was wrong about that business" |
| **Kraft Heinz** | 2015 | Overpayment | Acknowledged overpaying for brands |
| **IBM** | 2011 | ~$11B investment | Didn't produce expected returns; Buffett admitted he was wrong |

### What He Calls His Best Decisions

1. **Insurance float model** — The structural decision to use insurance premiums as investment capital
2. **GEICO** — A 70+ year relationship that became Berkshire's insurance cornerstone
3. **Coca-Cola (1988)** — Buying a global brand at a depressed price
4. **American Express (1964)** — The crisis investment that proved brand resilience
5. **Apple (2016)** — Breaking his own rules about tech to recognize a consumer ecosystem
6. **Washington Post (1973)** — ~100x return on a media monopoly
7. **Goldman Sachs (2008)** — Providing capital at the moment of maximum fear
8. **Hiring great managers** — Recurring theme in his letters; he credits managers like Ajit Jain and the late Tony Nicely

## 6. Decision-Making Patterns

### How Buffett Makes Investment Decisions

1. **Reading annual reports**: Buffett famously spends **5-6 hours a day reading**, primarily annual reports, 10-K filings, and industry publications. He reads hundreds of annual reports per year.

2. **Circle of competence**: He only invests in businesses he can understand:

> *"You only have to be able to evaluate companies within your circle of competence. The size of that circle is not very important; knowing its boundaries, however, is vital."*

3. **Management quality**: Buffett emphasizes investing in businesses so good that even an idiot could run them (because someday one will), but he also seeks out exceptional managers. He looks for **integrity, intelligence, and energy** in leadership.

4. **The "punch card" metaphor** (20 slots for lifetime investments):

> *"I could improve your ultimate financial welfare by giving you a ticket with only 20 slots in it, so that you had 20 punches — representing all the investments that you got to make in a lifetime. And once you'd punched through the card, you couldn't make any more investments at all. Under those rules, you'd really think carefully about what you did, and you'd be forced to load up on what you'd really thought about. So you'd do so much better."*

5. **Margin of safety**: Buy at a significant discount to intrinsic value to protect against errors in analysis.

6. **Long holding periods**: *"When we own portions of outstanding businesses with outstanding managements, our favorite holding period is forever."*

7. **Patience over activity**: *"Lethargy bordering on sloth remains the cornerstone of our investment style."*

8. **Avoiding leverage**: Buffett has long warned against debt. *"It's only when the tide goes out that you learn who has been swimming naked."*

### The Graham-to-Buffett Evolution

- **Phase 1 (Graham)**: "Cigar butt" investing — buying cheap, mediocre businesses at deep discounts to net asset value.
- **Phase 2 (Munger influence)**: Buying **wonderful businesses at fair prices** rather than fair businesses at cheap prices.
- **Phase 3 (Later Buffett)**: Recognizing that **great businesses with durable competitive advantages** ("moats") and excellent management deserve premium valuations.

This evolution is most visible in the progression from American Express (1964) to Coca-Cola (1988) to Apple (2016).

## 7. Consistency vs. Inconsistency

### "Buy and Hold Forever" vs. Selling Airlines in 2020
- Buffett has long advocated permanent holding periods for great businesses.
- Yet in April 2020, he sold all airline holdings at a loss, acknowledging: *"The world has changed for airlines."*
- **Resolution**: Buffett's "forever" qualifier is conditional — it applies to businesses with durable competitive advantages. When the thesis breaks (as he believed it did for airlines during COVID), he acts decisively.

### Avoiding Tech vs. Buying Apple
- For decades, Buffett avoided technology investments, saying they were outside his circle of competence.
- In 2011, he surprised observers by buying **$11 billion in IBM** — which didn't work out well.
- In 2016, he began buying Apple, which became his largest holding.
- **Resolution**: Buffett reframed Apple not as a tech stock but as a **consumer products company** with an ecosystem moat. His circle of competence expanded to include consumer technology with network effects.

### Avoiding Leverage vs. Using Insurance Float
- Buffett has repeatedly warned against the dangers of debt and leverage.
- Yet Berkshire's entire empire is built on **insurance float** — holding other people's money and investing it.
- **Resolution**: Insurance float is fundamentally different from debt. It has no fixed maturity, no covenants, and often costs less than nothing (when underwriting is profitable). Buffett distinguishes between "good leverage" (float) and "bad leverage" (debt with fixed obligations).

### Conservative Philosophy vs. Bold Crisis Bets
- Buffett is generally characterized as conservative and risk-averse.
- Yet during crises, he makes enormous concentrated bets: 40% of partnership assets in American Express (1964), $5B in Goldman Sachs (2008), $5B in Bank of America (2011).
- **Resolution**: Buffett is risk-averse about **permanent capital loss** but aggressive about **opportunity** when the odds are heavily in his favor. His conservatism is about process and margin of safety, not about avoiding all risk.
