# Patrick McKenzie (@patio11) — Writings Research

## Overview

Patrick McKenzie is an American software entrepreneur, writer, and advisor who lived in Japan for 20 years before relocating to Chicago. He is known online as **patio11**. He ran Kalzumeus Software (selling Bingo Card Creator and Appointment Reminder), co-founded Starfighter (game-based recruiting), worked at Stripe for roughly seven years (2016–2023), and now writes the Bits about Money newsletter about financial infrastructure. His blog at kalzumeus.com contains 575+ essays totaling 4.7 million words since 2006.

His self-described metric: "median value created for software people" multiplied by reach, largely from advising engineers to charge more. He estimates his salary negotiation essay alone generates roughly $7–9 million per year in marginal compensation improvement for readers.

## Part 1: Blog Posts — kalzumeus.com

### 1.1 "Don't Call Yourself A Programmer, And Other Career Advice" (Oct 2011)

**The post:** His most famous career essay, written as a "README.txt for your career" to fill gaps engineering education leaves unaddressed. It reframes how software engineers should think about their economic role.

**Core Thesis: Engineers Create Business Value, Not Software**

"Producing beautiful software is not a goal. Solving complex technical problems is not a goal." The only goals that matter are adding revenue and reducing costs.

About 90% of programming work is Line of Business (LOB) software — internal corporate tools for expense tracking, shipping optimization, accounting projections. These apps are often technically simple but economically valuable. He illustrates with a hypothetical internal expense reporting form at a 2,000-person company that could save $250,000 annually. The company doesn't care that it's "the world's simplest CRUD app" — only that it saves money.

**Profit Centers vs. Cost Centers**

Drawing on Peter Drucker's framework, McKenzie urges engineers to position themselves within Profit Centers — the parts of an organization that generate revenue — rather than being classified as Cost Centers. Profit Centers get "higher wages, more respect, and greater opportunities." Engineers are "usually very highly paid Cost Centers, which sets MBA's optimization antennae to twitching," which drives initiatives like outsourcing. "Nobody ever outsources Profit Centers."

**Don't Call Yourself a Programmer**

The label "programmer" frames you as a cost to be eliminated. Salesforce's entire sales pitch is essentially "No Software," signaling to clients they can "fire half" of their internal programming staff. Instead, describe yourself by what you've accomplished for employers in terms of revenue growth or cost reduction. Wall Street quants code but define themselves by outcomes — their work helps firms make billions.

**You Are Not Defined by Your Software Stack**

Debates about programming languages are irrelevant: "It doesn't matter. There you go." Labeling yourself as a "Java programmer" or ".NET programmer" is already a losing move. Picking up a new language takes weeks.

**Communication Is Your Most Important Skill**

The dominant quality that gets engineers jobs is "the ability to give people the perception that you will create value." Some excellent programmers can't carry a conversation and are underestimated; some merely good programmers communicate brilliantly and are overestimated. Be able to explain what you do to "a bright 8 year old, the CFO of your company, or a programmer in a different specialty."

**Modesty Is Not Career-Enhancing**

American businesses don't reward modesty. The right tone is "restrained, confident professionalism." Excessive modesty is like "a batter who feels poorly about stealing bases in baseball: you're not morally superior, you're just playing poorly."

**Startup Equity Realism**

On a d100, rolls 0–70 mean your equity is worth nothing, 71–94 means it roughly replaces what you gave up in salary, 95–99 means life-changing money, and 100 means you're at the next Google. He points out that 100 doesn't actually appear on a d100. Engineers "radically overestimate the likelihood that your startup will succeed and radically overestimate the portion of the pie" allocated to them.

**Final Wisdom**

"Family, faith, hobbies, etc etc generally swamp career achievements and money in terms of things which actually produce happiness." "Work to live, don't live to work."

**Key Frameworks:**
- Revenue/Cost lens: all career value flows from increasing revenue or reducing costs
- Profit Center vs. Cost Center positioning
- Communication > Technical skill for career advancement
- Network-first hiring (most good jobs are filled through relationships)
- Negotiation as career multiplier
- Startup equity realism
- Modesty is costly

### 1.2 "Salary Negotiation: Make More Money, Be More Valued" (Jan 2012)

**The post:** His negotiation guide, originally for engineers but widely applicable. Claims responsibility for roughly "$9 million a year in marginal improvement to compensation."

**Core Thesis**

Engineers and middle-class professionals systematically undervalue themselves in salary negotiations and treat this weakness as a virtue. Five minutes of negotiation can yield thousands of dollars that compound over an entire career.

**The Poetry Thought Experiment**

He opens by asking whether you'd recite poetry on one foot for $100,000. Obviously yes. Yet people refuse to spend five minutes negotiating salary — a similarly low-effort, high-reward activity.

**The $5,000 Compounding Framework**

A $5,000 raise doesn't just add $5,000 — it compounds annually through standard percentage-based raises, higher 401k contributions, and establishes a higher baseline for future job offers. Over ten years, a $5,000 annual increase approaches $100,000 gross.

**The Fully-Loaded Cost Framework**

Employees cost 150–200% of their salary when you account for taxes, benefits, healthcare, retirement contributions, and perks. This means $5,000 to you is roughly 3% of fully-loaded annual cost — essentially noise to the employer. "Virtually any amount of money available to you personally is mouse droppings to your prospective employer."

**Understanding "Bob" (The Hiring Manager)**

The person negotiating with you is spending the company's budget, not their own money. They typically receive no meaningful bonus for saving on your hire. "Bob is far, far less invested in this negotiation than you are."

**The Yes-If / No-But Framework**

Only negotiate after receiving a "Yes-If" — agreement in principle conditional on acceptable terms. Never negotiate from a "No-But" position where they might hire you if you're cheap enough. "Negotiating never makes (worthwhile) offers worse."

**Rule #1: Never Give a Number First**

When employers ask for salary history, desired range, or a specific number, they're anchoring against your previous (likely below-market) compensation. Volunteering a number signals incompetence to people with hiring authority.

**Repeat Their Words Back**

If the job description says "scaling traffic in a fast-moving environment," use those exact phrases to describe your experience. Take notes during interviews to capture their specific phrasing.

**Multi-Dimensional Negotiation**

Don't fixate on salary alone. Compensation includes vacation days, work hours, project assignments, remote work flexibility, professional development, equity, and more. Trade improvements across dimensions.

**Key Quotes:**
- "We overwhelmingly suck at it. We have turned sucking at it into a perverse badge of virtue."
- "Your salary negotiations are probably going to be the most important financial decisions you will ever make."
- "You're not a peasant. Don't act like one."
- "You can trivially pick up $5,000 in salary negotiations just by sucking less."
- "You never need a job. Being needy means that the party who is not needy has automatic leverage."
- "Rich, successful people negotiate. This is one important way in which they get — and stay — rich."
- "resumes are an institution created to mean that no one has to read resumes."

### 1.3 "Running A Software Business On 5 Hours A Week" (Mar 2010)

**The post:** Chronicles running Bingo Card Creator while working full-time as a Japanese salaryman in Nagoya. The business succeeded enough to allow him to quit his day job.

**Time Assets vs. Time Debt**

The central framework. Time Assets are things you build today that save you time indefinitely — code, systems, processes, scalable marketing, documentation that preemptively answers customer questions. Time Debt is any action that commits you to unavoidable future work. "Time *can* be stored."

**Code Is ~10% of the Business (Maybe Less)**

The vast majority of effort and results come from non-coding activities: marketing, SEO, customer support, copywriting, pre-sales inquiries. His product was essentially "Hello World attached to a random number generator." Customers care about solutions to problems, not technical sophistication.

**Relentlessly Cut Scope**

Every line of code is time debt requiring debugging, support, documentation, and potential future rewrites. "Cutting your feature set to the bone is the single best advice I can give you." "'complete' is 100% a marketing decision that the software as it exists is Good Enough."

**People Don't Buy Software**

"People don't buy software because of what it does, they buy it for the positive change it will make." Sell benefits over features.

**Avoid Publicly Visible Deadlines**

The only acceptable response to a feature request: "'Thank you for your feedback. I will take it under advisement.'" Committing to schedules is itself time debt.

**Calendar Time vs. Wall Clock Time**

Calendar time (days/months where time must pass for something to happen) is essentially free since you're planning to exist next week anyway. A/B testing requires only 15 minutes of setup but weeks of waiting — ideal for part-time schedules.

**Avoid Events, Plan For Processes**

One-time efforts deliver fleeting results. Build repeatable processes: systematic outreach, repeatable linkbait, systematic ways to encourage word-of-mouth.

**Key Quotes:**
- "DO NOT do work on your business at your day job" — his "iron law"
- "always, always, always trust the data over me if the data says I'm wrong"
- "Talk to a carpenter, ask him what about carpentry sucks" — on finding business ideas

### 1.4 "How Running A Business Changes The Way You Think" (Jul 2011)

**The post:** Reflections on psychological transformation through entrepreneurship.

**Self-Knowledge About Motivation**

He identifies himself as a "praise-seeking missile" whose early academic success was driven by dopamine from approval, not love of learning. Money was never his primary motivator — he was fascinated by money as a system to optimize, not as something to accumulate. "If you have a good handle on what really motivates you, you probably have a good handle on things which do not."

**The Career Preference Space Framework**

Careers exist in a multi-dimensional preference space with thousands of variables: income, location, hours, happiness, autonomy, meaning. The straight-line narrative connecting hard work to success is "a _story_. A narrative."

**The World Is Not a Meritocracy**

"**The world isn't a meritocracy.**" The conventional career narrative of good grades → good college → good job → rewards is a "cracking good story" but not a reliable description of reality.

**Everything Is Negotiable**

Most "rules" about work are actually just conventions. Any two parties who agree on something can make it happen. "If Bob wants it, and asks for it, and gets it agreed to, Bob gets what he wants." "You can often get what you want."

**Company Loyalty Is a Constructed Value**

"stuff company loyalty" — companies are "legal fictions" for moving capital; loyalty should be reserved for people.

**Money as a Tool, Not a Goal**

As his business grew, his relationship with money shifted from scarcity-thinking (agonizing over $5 purchases) to strategic deployment. He stopped doing tasks he disliked (like web design) and started paying others, calculating that his time was better spent on higher-value work.

**The Confidence Gap and Underpricing**

Thomas Ptacek told him a coffee chat was worth $15,000, while McKenzie would have been happy with $300. His first consulting rate: typing a number, raising it 50%, then hitting send while hyperventilating — only to have the client accept without complaint. "gradually getting better at the confidence thing. Partially, it is a result of trying new things."

**The Japanese Salaryman Lesson**

Working 70–90 hour weeks in a Japanese megacorp crystallized that how we work is essentially arbitrary. The 40-hour week with decent pay is no more a "law of nature" than the 90-hour week with poor pay.

### 1.5 "Doing Business In Japan" (Nov 2014)

**The post:** 20 years of experience distilled into a comprehensive guide. His most culturally specific work.

**The Salaryman-Employer Relationship**

The core framework. About one-third of the labor force are salarymen (正社員), but they dominate the high-productivity private sector. The deal: employees swear themselves to the company body and soul; in return, the company isolates them from all risk.

Employee obligations: first obligation in all things is to the company. 90+ hour weeks barely occasion comment. The company can reassign you abroad immediately. The company molds employees regardless of educational background — "if your company needs a Java-speaking systems engineer and you have a degree in Art History, this is *not a problem*." The social expectation is to never quit — mid-career hires are extremely rare, and leaving brands you as "damaged goods."

Company obligations: provides structure, purpose, identity, and respect. Handles taxes, housing, insurance, pension, paperwork — even immigration matters. Attempts to arrange marriage matches for employees. Boss gives the longest speech at your wedding.

Robert Locke (2005) wrote that Japanese employers "allocate respect directly" rather than through large monetary compensation.

**Startups Are Considered Extremely Risky**

Being self-employed has "numerous negative impacts on your life." McKenzie was asked to leave his apartment after quitting his megacorp job because "self-employed is about one half-step above homeless vagabond" in social esteem. Most young women and parents prefer salarymen for marriage stability. He brought his resume and tax returns when meeting his mother-in-law.

**Hiring: Exploiting Flaws in Salarymandom**

Startups must hire "around the edges" of Japanese society. One hiring manager said he looks for "Otaku, Koreans, foreigners, dropouts" — McKenzie notes that "at least hope to run into ruthlessly capitalist racists, because that's something you can work with."

**The State of Modern Web in Japan**

Japan is roughly "5~10 years behind the skill curve relative to the US" for web/mobile development. Best-in-class Japanese web apps from 2014 "asymptotically approach Facebook 1.0 in functionality."

**The Personal Touch: The Bank Story (Taro)**

A regional bank manager named Taro correctly intuited McKenzie's dating life from spending patterns. The day after the 2011 earthquake, Taro called to say "we'll take care of you and worry about the numbers some other time." When Taro retired, he gave handwritten notes and a warm introduction to his successor.

**Racism**

"Is racism a bigger problem in Japan than in the United States? Oh, yes. Unquestionably." ~40% of available apartments in Ogaki bounced him for being foreign. "Highly-paid well-educated articulate Western men with deep Japanese social networks are *almost* Japanese."

**Language**

Running a business without Japanese "strikes me as playing life on hard mode." "Learning to speak, read, and write Japanese is enormously fun. So is starting a company. I recommend not combining the two."

**Is Japan Good for Business?**

The market is "tremendously underserved" for technology solutions. UX that Silicon Valley considers barely adequate would strike Japanese customers as "wizardry from the future." Dave McClure noted Japanese startups are too eager to exit Japan when the domestic market is gigantic and "the competition usually sucks."

### 1.6 "Falsehoods Programmers Believe About Names" (Jun 2010)

**The post:** Identified a common class of application design bugs, spawning an entire genre of "Falsehoods programmers believe about" content across the industry.

Having worked with global enterprises, McKenzie states he has "never seen a computer system which handles names properly and doubt one exists, anywhere." He calls names "central to our identities."

The post enumerates 40 assumptions systems make about names, all wrong. People don't have one canonical full name, don't have exactly N names for any fixed N, names aren't limited to ASCII, names aren't globally unique, and names aren't necessarily assigned at birth.

The final punchline: even the assumption that "people have names" is sometimes wrong. The sardonic note that people whose names break systems should have had "solid, acceptable names, like 田中太郎" reveals his cross-cultural perspective.

### 1.7 "You Can Probably Stand To Charge More" (Aug 2006)

**The post:** His earliest and most concise pricing advice.

**Key Ideas:**
- Don't volunteer how long it took to build your product. The heart surgeon analogy: doctors don't itemize the cost of plastic and chips when billing $50,000.
- Non-developers will never replicate your software. To them, computers are "black magic" and you are "the shaman they rely on for magical amulets."
- Price based on value delivered, not cost to create. The demand curve is external to your development effort — "it doesn't read your time cards."
- Software founders undervalue their products because they see every wart, bug, and compromise daily. They compare the real product to the idealized one. "Stop."
- At least one product he knew "would probably see **higher** sales if it were priced at _triple_."

### 1.8 "Doubling SaaS Revenue By Changing The Pricing Model" (Aug 2012)

**The post:** Case study of Server Density moving from variable per-server pricing to tiered plan pricing, achieving a 100% increase in revenue per visitor.

**Key Frameworks:**

Linear pricing misaligns with exponential customer value. A company with 7 servers isn't getting 7x the value of a 1-server customer — they may get 50x or more when revenue, on-call staff costs, and reputational risk are factored in.

**How to Read Any SaaS Pricing Grid:**
1. Largest plan → generates 33–50% of gross revenue (sold to people spending corporate budgets)
2. Most popular plan → nobody likes being the cheapskate
3. Highest support cost → the cheapest/free plan attracts the most demanding customers
4. Free plan → attracts pathological customers with unreasonable expectations

The A/B test: variable pricing won on trial signups, but plan pricing **doubled actual revenue** despite fewer signups.

**Key Quotes:**
- "most customers do not care about price"
- "Those that do are disproportionately terrible customers."
- "Virtually no one gets pricing right on the first try."
- "You raise prices, and you deal with less crazy people."

McKenzie removed a $9 plan from Appointment Reminder and added a $199 plan instead — both moves were "nothing but win."

### 1.9 "Marketing Software, For People Who Would Rather Be Building It" (Apr 2013)

**The post:** His comprehensive marketing framework for engineers.

**Core Philosophy:** "a job is a system that turns time into money, and a business is a system that turns systems into money." Engineers have a natural advantage because they think in systems.

**The "Rock in the Lake" Framework:** The market is a placid lake representing current equilibrium. New technology or information drops are rocks creating ripples. "The larger the ripple is, the more change there is, the more possibility there is to get outsized profits."

**Customer Discovery — The Massage Therapist Story:** After generating 400 ideas for Twilio-powered products, McKenzie discovered Appointment Reminder through a real-world encounter. A massage therapist told him her hands-on-a-phone meant hands-not-on-backs, meaning lost revenue.

**"Mom Test" Validation:** If someone says "shut up and take my money," you have a real problem. If they say "that's kind of interesting, tell me when that exists," you have not successfully identified a real problem.

**Email vs. RSS:** "Email is _actually read_. RSS is not read."

**First-Run Software Experience:** 40–60% of users never return after their first session. Script the first five minutes "like it is the invasion of Normandy." The World of Warcraft Principle: teach gameplay through guided micro-tasks with immediate feedback.

**Key Quote:** "If you have me in charge of your marketing, you need to fire me, and your sales will go up by 50 percent." — his sales improved dramatically when he stopped tinkering with Bingo Card Creator.

## Part 2: Starfighter

### 2.1 Announcing Starfighter (Mar 2015)

**What it was:** A company co-founded by Patrick McKenzie, Thomas Ptacek, and Erin Ptacek. Published CTF (Capture The Flag) games designed to develop and assess rare programming skills. Totally free for players — "You will code to play. You will not pay to code."

**Core Thesis:** "We're not here to fix the technical interview: we're here to destroy it."

Work-sample tests are the scientifically validated best predictor of job performance, but companies can't build them. Starfighter solved this by being "a contingency recruiter with access to a better way to identify candidates."

**Critique of Traditional Hiring:**
- "Your candidate filter greps resumes rather than ability"
- Donald Knuth wouldn't pass resume screening for a "senior C programmer with Unix experience" role
- Traditional interviews are "secretive, scary, and stressful for candidates"
- The industry "structurally excludes many qualified candidates" including those from underrepresented backgrounds

**Business Model:** Contingency recruiting — companies pay a percentage of first-year salary upon successful hire. "Persistent market inefficiencies should be music to the ears of a capitalist, because they suggest free money."

**What Starfighter reveals about McKenzie's thinking:**
- He applies systems thinking to broken processes (hiring) and designs game-like solutions
- He's willing to attack established institutions directly ("destroy the technical interview")
- He identifies structural inefficiencies as business opportunities
- He builds on past successes (MicroCorruption CTF) rather than starting from scratch
- He self-identifies as "practically the non-technical co-founder" — honest about his relative strengths

## Part 3: Appointment Reminder

### 3.1 Origin Story

McKenzie discovered the idea through real-world customer discovery. A massage therapist told him her hands-on-a-phone meant hands-not-on-backs, meaning lost revenue. He then walked around downtown Chicago paying massage therapists for their time but asking business questions instead. He asked whether they'd pay $30/month for automated reminders and collected emails.

### 3.2 Pricing Lessons

His own mistake: the $9/month "Personal" plan attracted 80% of support requests, 95% of headaches, churned at double the rate of the $29 plan, and generated almost no revenue. A single Professional signup was worth more than six Personal signups.

He removed the $9 plan and added a $199 plan — both moves were "nothing but win."

**Key insight on tiers:** His Japanese boss refused to request reimbursement for a "Hobbyist" plan, upgrading to "Enterprise" because he wouldn't present anything called "hobbyist" to his superiors. Lesson: naming tiers matters enormously.

### 3.3 Marketing Automation

His most common consulting deliverable was a lifecycle email campaign for first-time users, routinely increasing conversion by ~15%. Quick win: email month-to-month SaaS customers offering an annual plan upgrade with a modest discount — "it routinely gets 20%+ uptake."

### 3.4 First-Run Experience

Appointment Reminder's tour mode calls the user to demo the reminder experience — a guided walkthrough that eliminates the blank-screen problem. Instrumented everything with funnel analytics (KISSmetrics). A specific intervention on Bingo Card Creator's funnel increased successful card completion from 60% to 70%. A separate two-hour optimization effort durably increased sales by 16%.

### 3.5 What Appointment Reminder reveals about McKenzie's thinking

- He validates ideas through direct customer conversation, not assumptions
- He treats pricing as an ongoing experiment, not a one-time decision
- He applies engineering discipline (instrumentation, A/B testing) to marketing
- He's willing to cut products/features that don't work, even when they have users
- He builds systems (email campaigns, funnels) rather than doing one-off marketing

## Part 4: Stripe

### 4.1 Joining Stripe (Sep 2016)

McKenzie joined at roughly employee #650, primarily to work on Stripe Atlas. His central framework for career decisions is **leverage** — "things which increase your capability to provide impact." His success function: impact = (number of people helped) × (delta in average life uniquely caused by your efforts). He joined Stripe because it offered far more leverage than running solo businesses.

### 4.2 Two Years at Stripe (Mar 2019)

**On Ambition:** The biggest belief shift — "ambition properly harnessed can be an enormously productive force in the world." He distinguishes between treating a credit card processor as "impossible" versus recognizing it as "an enumerable set of regulations and writing a finite number of lines of code."

**The Senior IC Path:** McKenzie is an individual contributor, not a manager. He argues companies should offer explicit IC career tracks so people aren't forced into management. Senior ICs create value through strategic direction-setting, mentoring (including managers), building systems and processes, and making others more effective.

**Writing Culture:** Stripe produces enormous volumes of internal writing. He reframes strategy memos as "a pull request for a very complicated program which directs the activities of dozens or hundreds of people."

**Code as a Solution to Human Problems:** "the right way to do all stylistic arguments is to enforce them with code." Champions heavy investment in internal tooling, emphasizing composability.

**Hypergrowth (The "2X" Framework):** At 2X annual growth, half your coworkers always have less than one year of tenure. Historical numbers become "glaringly wrong" almost immediately. You must write documentation for people who aren't at the company yet.

**Quality as an Organizational Choice:** On why Stripe's product pages look exceptional: "The reason those pages are phenomenal is that a very small group of very talented people care an almost unreasonable amount."

**"Nothing is Not My Job"** — his favorite aspect of Stripe's culture.

### 4.3 Four Years at Stripe (Oct 2020)

His work split into three roughly equal buckets: project work, consultative/community engagement, and non-core experiments.

**On Hypergrowth:** Stripe grew from ~650 to ~3,000 employees. "Stripe is, qualitatively and quantitatively, about as different counting from my start date to today as it was counting from launch to my start date."

**Operating Cadence:** Stripe consistently chooses to move faster. A simple management technique: asking "Could we do that faster? What is the minimum increment required to ship?" repeatedly in meetings.

**Economic Model:** He considers Stripe's business model superior to typical B2B SaaS, describing it as indexing on the success of companies that themselves index on their customers' success.

**On Being an Employee vs. Entrepreneur:** His metric: "making a large improvement in the lives of a large number of software people." He still constantly feels the pull to start another company but sees Stripe as the best vehicle for his long-term goals.

**2020 Struggles:** Open about experiencing severe depression. Medication and daily exercise produced "a revolutionary improvement in QOL."

### 4.4 What Stripe reveals about McKenzie's thinking

- He chose organizational leverage over personal autonomy — a significant shift for a bootstrapper
- He values ambition as a force multiplier, not just a personality trait
- He sees writing as organizational infrastructure, not just communication
- He advocates for IC career paths — challenging the management-track orthodoxy
- He applies systems thinking to organizations themselves ("programming for organizations")
- He's honest about mental health struggles

## Part 7: Cross-Cutting Themes and Thinking Patterns

### 7.1 SaaS Business Models and Pricing Strategy

**Core beliefs:**
- Charge more. This is his most repeated advice across all writings.
- Value-based pricing > cost-based pricing. Price based on what the customer gains, not what you spent.
- Non-linear pricing captures more value. A 1,000-person company has orders of magnitude more money than 1,000 one-person companies.
- Packaging matters more than price tweaks. Radical restructuring beats ±$10 adjustments.
- Cheap customers are disproportionately terrible. They generate the most support, churn fastest, and complain loudest.
- Free plans attract pathological customers.
- A/B test pricing. Grandfather existing customers.
- The largest pricing tier generates 33–50% of revenue.

**Key pricing quotes:**
- "Charge more, charge more, charge more, charge more, charge more."
- "most customers do not care about price. Those that do are disproportionately terrible customers."
- "Virtually no one gets pricing right on the first try."
- "You raise prices, and you deal with less crazy people."

### 7.2 Software Economics

**LTV, CAC, churn, margins:**
- Software marginal cost approaches zero, letting you choose your price point on the demand curve.
- Customer loyalty in SaaS is high once locked in — switching costs work both ways.
- Automated marketing email routinely increases conversion by ~15%.
- Annual plan upgrades from monthly: "it routinely gets 20%+ uptake."
- The fully-loaded cost of an employee is 150–200% of salary — making SaaS tools that save even 1–2 hours a no-brainer purchase.
- At $200/month, you represent ~5% of the cheapest fully-loaded employee cost.

### 7.3 Marketing for Software Products

**Core philosophy:** Every software company should have full-time engineers working on their marketing funnel. Virtually none do. Many of his biggest wins came from shipping simple engineering artifacts that affected funnel metrics by 3–15%.

**SEO strategy:** Before launch, map out a content strategy. "Snowflake queries" (one-time Google searches) — create as many pages as possible, each targeting a narrow interest. Scaled content creation using Rails and freelancers, eventually accounting for ~50% of sales and ~75% of profits.

**Email marketing:** Email is "actually read." RSS is not. His email campaign arc: early emails focus on trust-building, middle emails gradually increase sales intensity, peak pitch eliminates all decision-making except yes/no.

**First-run experience:** 40–60% of users never return after first session. Script the first five minutes "like it is the invasion of Normandy." Pre-populate data, use guided tours, "tell people they're having fun now."

### 7.4 The Indie Hacker / Bootstrapper Perspective

**Time management:** Time Assets vs. Time Debt. Code is ~10% of the business. Calendar time is essentially free. Avoid events, plan for processes.

**Validation:** Talk to real customers. "If someone says 'shut up and take my money,' you have a real problem." Use the "Mom Test" — ask business questions, not feature questions.

**Scope:** Cut the feature set to the bone. "'complete' is 100% a marketing decision."

**Day job compatibility:** "DO NOT do work on your business at your day job." Get your employer onboard by framing it as a programming hobby that builds skills.

**Consulting positioning:** Don't compete as a generic developer. Position as someone who increases revenue. "If you can credibly claim to double a company's $10M in sales, you're cheap compared to $10M in marginal revenue."

### 7.5 Software Engineering as a Career

**Core reframing:** You are not a "programmer." You are a person who solves business problems with software. Frame your work in terms of money made or saved.

**Career advice:**
- Position yourself in Profit Centers, not Cost Centers
- Communication > technical skill
- Negotiate salary aggressively — it compounds over a career
- Networking fills most desirable positions before any formal HR process begins
- Your resume is a sales document, not a biography
- Modesty is not career-enhancing
- Startup equity is usually worth nothing

**On ambition:** "ambition properly harnessed can be an enormously productive force in the world." Surround yourself with ambitious peers — they "directly intervene on the ambition of impressionable people."

### 7.6 Japan and Japanese Business Culture

**The salaryman system:** Employees swear themselves to the company; the company isolates them from all risk. This is "almost a Rosetta stone" for understanding Japanese business.

**Cultural observations:**
- "allocate respect directly" rather than through large monetary compensation
- "self-employed is about one half-step above homeless vagabond" in social esteem
- 90-hour weeks but very low output due to process-heavy culture
- "Learning to speak, read, and write Japanese is enormously fun. So is starting a company. I recommend not combining the two."

**On racism:** "Is racism a bigger problem in Japan than in the United States? Oh, yes. Unquestionably."

**Business opportunity:** The market is "tremendously underserved." UX that Silicon Valley considers barely adequate would strike Japanese customers as "wizardry from the future."

### 8.1 Verbose and Precise

McKenzie writes at extraordinary length — 4.7 million words across 575+ essays. His posts are structured, thorough, and leave no ambiguity. He provides specific numbers, concrete examples, and actionable frameworks rather than vague advice.

### 8.2 Analytical and Systems-Oriented

### 8.3 Cross-Cultural Perspective

### 8.7 Action-Oriented
