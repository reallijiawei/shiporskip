# Paul Graham's Expression DNA

Research into PG's writing style, rhetorical devices, and communication patterns.
Based on direct analysis of 9 essays from paulgraham.com (2004-2024).

---

## 1. Essay Structure: The Architecture

PG's essays follow a consistent structural arc, though they never feel formulaic.

**Typical progression:**
1. Provocative opening (paradox, question, or contrarian claim)
2. Background observation or personal experience
3. Problem framing (often through contrast or negative space)
4. Core argument built through examples and analogies
5. Addressing objections (preemptive concession-then-pivot)
6. Broader implications or generalization
7. Short, punchy closing that echoes the opening

**Section style:** Essays use short, evocatively labeled sections -- single words or short phrases ("Trees," "Corn Syrup," "Filters," "Well") rather than descriptive academic headings. Each section stands somewhat independently but builds cumulative force.

**Paragraph discipline:** Almost never exceeds 3-4 sentences. Many paragraphs are a single sentence used for emphasis. The one-sentence paragraph is a signature move:

> "Startups don't win by attacking. They win by transcending."

> "Choose the latter."

> "Just build things."

**Length range:** Essays vary from ~1,000 words ("The Top of My Todo List") to ~10,000+ words ("How to Do Great Work"), but the prose stays equally compressed regardless of length. Long essays are long because they cover many facets, not because they pad.

## 2. Sentence Patterns: How He Builds Arguments

### The Short Declarative Anchor

PG's default sentence is short, punchy, subject-verb-object. These function as thesis statements or landing punches after longer explanatory passages:

> "Wealth is the fundamental thing."

> "Beware of research."

> "You need both."

> "There isn't."

He alternates these with longer explanatory sentences, creating a rhythm of assertion-and-expansion.

### The Assertion-Qualification-Reassertion Cycle

A signature PG move: state a claim plainly, acknowledge the counterargument, then return to the claim with more nuance. Bold claim → "But..." → refined restatement.

> "I wouldn't say founders seem happier, necessarily; starting a startup can be very stressful." [qualification] ...founders are happier "in the sense that your body is happier during a long run than sitting on a sofa eating doughnuts." [reassertion]

### The Contrarian Reframe

PG frequently sets up conventional wisdom, then flips it with a single pivot:

> "The way to get startup ideas is not to try to think of startup ideas."

> "The verb you want to be using... is not 'think up' but 'notice.'"

> "It seems presumptuous to try to be Newton or Shakespeare... But that's what's going on subconsciously."

### Rhetorical Questions as Transitions

He uses questions to pivot between sections or introduce new angles:

> "Why is it so important to work on a problem you have?"

> "Is that so bad?"

> "How do you recognize these?"

> "Could that possibly be a coincidence?"

He then answers them directly, often with a blunt one-liner.

### The Hypothetical/Conditional Frame

Rather than flat assertions, PG often builds through conditionals that give the reader room to assent:

> "If you wanted to get rich, how would you do it?"

> "If you want to do great things, you'll have to work very hard."

> "Suppose you own a beat-up old car."

### Dialogue with an Imagined Reader

He addresses a hypothetical skeptic directly, creating intimacy and anticipating objections:

> "Sounds a bit extreme, you think."

> "How bad could it be?"

### Parenthetical Self-Correction

PG interrupts his own sentences to sharpen claims mid-flight:

> "I could see myself -- _can_ see myself -- making at least 4 of these 5 mistakes."

### The Periodic (Delayed-Clause) Sentence

For climactic moments, he builds through parallel phrases before landing the main point -- a classic periodic structure that creates suspense and weight.

## 3. Analogies and Metaphors: Where He Draws From

PG's analogies are his primary rhetorical engine. They are vivid, concrete, and drawn from physical experience.

### Source Domains

**Biology and nature:** Lions in the wild vs. zoo animals. Mosquitoes vs. bears. Curiosity as both "engine and rudder." Morale as "a living organism."

**Everyday objects and food:** High fructose corn syrup for big-company jobs. Doughnuts and pizza for short-term satisfaction. A magic machine that makes cars and cooks dinner.

> "A startup is like a mosquito. A bear can absorb a hit and a crab is armored against one, but a mosquito is designed for one thing: to score."

**Mechanical/engineering:** A car spinning its wheels. An angle grinder throwing off sparks. An engine with a low-restriction exhaust system.

**Physical geography:** The Meander River for essay structure. Wells vs. craters for demand shape. Mountains vs. plains for startup terrain.

**Military and strategy:** Guerrilla fighters preferring difficult terrain. Ancient army skirmishers for fighting orthodoxy. Staying upwind. Depth-first search.

**Mathematics and physics:** Simultaneous equations. Activation energy for starting work. Exponential growth. Fractals for knowledge edges.

**Historical/cultural:** Mapmakers putting mistakes in maps. Comb-overs illustrating incremental absurdity. Bell-bottom jeans for moral fashion.

### How the Analogies Work

PG never starts with the abstraction. The pattern: abstract claim → physical image → "that's the point." The analogies are **brief** -- they land and move on. He draws from STEM fields more than literary ones, reinforcing his ethos as a technical thinker writing about creativity.

## 4. Certainty vs. Uncertainty: The Calibration

PG navigates confidence with notable care. He is not uniformly bold -- he modulates deliberately.

### Where He Is Certain

He uses flat declaratives for claims backed by broad empirical observation:

> "The empirical evidence is on the scale of the evidence for mortality."

> "Empirically, the way to have good startup ideas is to become the sort of person who has them."

> "Nearly all good startup ideas are of that type."

He is most confident about **negative** claims -- what doesn't work, what to avoid. He states these as near-universal laws.

### Where He Is Uncertain

He uses explicit hedging and flags the limits of his knowledge openly:

> "I don't know if it's possible to cultivate originality."

> "You can't with certainty."

> "I'm willing to believe."

> "I don't know enough to say."

He hedges with "probably," "often," "in most cases," and "presumably" rather than universalizing.

### The "I Think" Pattern

PG uses "I think" not as a hedge but as an ownership marker. It signals personal conviction rather than uncertainty:

> "I think your best bet would be to start or join a startup."

> "I think there are several reasons."

### Structural Confidence

He often frames claims as near-universal laws, qualifying only in footnotes. The main text takes clear positions; the notes handle exceptions. This keeps the prose decisive without being dishonest.

### Acknowledging Paradox

Rather than resolving tensions, PG presents them:

> "Strictness plus rule-breaking sounds like a strange combination."

> "Never give up is also not quite right."

He treats paradoxes as genuine, not as problems to dissolve.

## 5. Humor Style

PG's humor is dry, understated, and embedded in logic rather than setup-punchline structure.

### Types of Humor

**Absurdist juxtaposition:** Describing his wife Jessica's character-reading ability as "like standing next to an airport baggage scanner."

**Understatement applied to the obvious:** "It seems quite likely that most successful drug lords are mean." The hedging ("seems quite likely") applied to something obviously true is where the humor lives.

**Deadpan deflation:** "Coprophiles, as of this writing, don't seem to be numerous or energetic enough to have had their interests promoted to a lifestyle."

**Self-deprecation:** Admitting he was "about as observant as a lump of rock" at sixteen. Calling himself "comically eager to sell."

**Reframing for absurdity:** "In an artificial world, only extremists live naturally" -- funny because it reframes health-conscious people as revolutionaries.

**The footnote swerve:** Sudden tonal shifts in footnotes, like "do not finance your startup with credit cards" -- blunt, almost parental advice dropped into an otherwise philosophical essay.

**Running gags:** The "time machine" motif in "What You Can't Say" repeated as structural device, becoming almost a joke through repetition.

### What the Humor Does

The humor never derails the argument. It punctuates transitions, releases tension after uncomfortable claims, and makes serious points memorable. It serves argumentative purposes rather than existing for its own sake.

## 6. High-Frequency Words and Phrases

### Signature Phrases

- **"The trick is..."** -- introducing a key insight
- **"The problem is..."** -- naming an obstacle
- **"Notice that..."** -- drawing attention to something the reader might miss
- **"Empirically..."** -- grounding claims in observed patterns
- **"I think..."** -- ownership marker, not hedge
- **"The way to X is not to X"** -- contrarian reframe structure
- **"In practice..."** -- distinguishing theory from reality
- **"At YC we found..."** -- institutional authority from experience
- **"It struck me that..."** -- personal observation as evidence

### High-Frequency Words

PG's vocabulary is deliberately plain. His most-used words are common English:

- **people, thing, way, make, want, seem, think, work, problem, start, idea, good, know, find, time, probably, actually**

He uses "thing" more than most writers -- it signals compression (he's naming a complex concept with the simplest possible word) and informality.

### Connective Patterns

- **"But"** is his most important conjunction -- it signals the concession-then-pivot pattern
- **"And yet"** -- creates rhythm of claim-then-complication
- **"Or rather"** -- self-correction mid-sentence
- **"That is"** -- restating more precisely
- **"In other words"** -- translating his own abstraction into concrete terms

## 7. Simplifying Complex Ideas

PG has a specific technique set for making complex ideas accessible.

**Sequential simplification:** He delivers sophisticated ideas one simple claim at a time. Each sentence adds one layer. He never piles complexity.

**The analogy shortcut:** Instead of explaining a mechanism, he maps it to something the reader already understands. One sentence does the work of a paragraph.

**Concrete before abstract:** He always introduces concrete examples before abstracting to principles. When discussing zero-sum to positive-sum economies, he starts with Archimedes and railroad monopolies, then generalizes.

**Compression through framing:** He reduces complex history to causal chains. In "The Age of the Essay," he traces modern essay assignments back through German research universities, English professors, classical scholarship, and medieval disputation -- all in a few paragraphs by finding the single hinge in each era.

**Appositional restatement:** He says something, then restates it more bluntly. After explaining wealth vs. money at length: "Wealth is what you want, not money." The second version is always simpler.

**The "sketch" move:** He signals approximation explicitly: "Here is a brief sketch" -- giving himself permission to simplify without losing credibility.

**Inverting lists:** He takes someone else's list, compresses it to one idea, then flips it into actionable commands:

> "Don't ignore your dreams; don't work too much; say what you think; cultivate friendships; be happy."

**Translating jargon:** He never uses discipline-specific vocabulary without immediately translating it. Latin phrases get parenthetical translations. Technical concepts get everyday equivalents.

## 8. Openings and Closes

### Opening Patterns

**The contrarian paradox:** Open by stating the opposite of what the reader expects.

> "The way to get startup ideas is not to try to think of startup ideas."

**The personal question:** Ground an abstract essay in a universal concrete experience.

> "Have you ever seen an old photo of yourself and been embarrassed at the way you looked?"

> "If you wanted to get rich, how would you do it?"

**The grand thesis:** Start with a principle that frames everything.

> "Technology tends to separate normal from natural."

**The disarming qualifier:** Establish method before claims.

> "If you collected lists of techniques for doing great work in a lot of different fields, what would the intersection look like?"

**The understated observation:** A personal epiphany delivered without fanfare.

> "It struck me recently how few of the most successful people I know are mean."

**The shared memory:** Address the reader as a peer.

> "Remember the essays you had to write in high school?"

What PG does NOT do in openings: No "In this essay I will argue..." No scene-setting or throat-clearing. He's in the argument within two sentences.

### Closing Patterns

**The short kicker:** A one-line closing that echoes the opening thesis.

> "Do you want to do great work, or not?"

**The self-demonstrating move:** The essay performs its own argument. "The Age of the Essay" meanders following curiosity, proving its own thesis.

**The agency shift:** End by turning responsibility to the reader.

> "Why not by you?"

**The escalating implication:** Build outward from specific advice to sweeping, almost prophetic claims.

**The admission of surprise:** Reveal that the essay's conclusion surprised even the author.

What PG does NOT do in closings: No summaries. No "In conclusion." No neat bows. The ending opens outward rather than closing down.

## 9. Rhetorical Devices

### Rhetorical Questions

Used constantly to anticipate reader objections, introduce new angles, and create conversational rhythm:

> "Why do startups have to be small?"

> "If the answer is no, you might want to stop and think about that."

### Lists and Tricolons

Lists of three appear throughout, building momentum:

> "food, clothes, houses, cars, gadgets, travel to interesting places"

> "groups of 8 work well; by 20 they're getting hard to manage; and a group of 50 is really unwieldy"

### Contrast Pairs

He frequently sets up two things and differentiates them:

- wealth vs. money
- measurement vs. leverage
- small vs. large companies
- writers vs. think-nots
- wild vs. zoo

### Anaphora and Repetition

Repetition with variation creates rhythm and emphasis:

> "Don't do as you're told. Don't believe what you're supposed to."

He returns to core themes from different angles, creating a fractal argument structure.

### The Concession-Then-Pivot

His most-used structural move. He acknowledges the counterargument before deepening rather than retreating:

> "It might not seem there's much to learn about how to work hard" → then systematically demonstrates otherwise.

> "Sounds a bit extreme, you think" → then explains why it's not.

### Chiasmus and Inversion

Occasional classical structures for emphasis:

> "The most important thing is to be able to think what you want, not to say what you want."

### Reductio ad Absurdum

He uses hypothetical extremes to expose mechanisms -- like inventing an absurd future taboo (the "yellow" hypothetical) to make the mechanism of taboo recognizable without triggering readers' defenses.

### Name-Dropping as Evidence

He anchors abstract claims in famous examples -- Bill Gates, Messi, Wodehouse, Newton, Shakespeare. Each name does double duty: proving the claim and lending borrowed gravitas. He doesn't argue why these people succeeded; he lets their reputations do the persuasive work.

### The Essay Performing Its Argument

His most powerful device: the essay's structure mirrors its thesis. "The Age of the Essay" meanders following curiosity, proving essays should explore. "How to Do Great Work" demonstrates sustained attention through its own length.

### Footnotes as Escape Valves

Controversial or tangential points get pushed to notes, keeping the main text clean. The footnotes add nuance; the main text takes clear positions.

## 10. Anti-Patterns: What PG Does NOT Do

**No jargon:** No "synergy," "paradigm," "value proposition." Everything in plain English a smart teenager could understand.

**No passive voice as default:** Almost every sentence has a clear subject doing something. Things happen because specific actors do them.

**No appeals to unnamed experts:** Never "studies suggest" or "experts agree." He either cites specifics (Bill Gates said X) or says "I think."

**No throat-clearing:** He's in the argument within two sentences. No "In this essay I will argue..." No preamble.

**No false balance:** He doesn't say "some might argue the Pie Fallacy has merit." He names it, explains why it's wrong, moves on.

**No management-speak:** Instead of "optimize organizational leverage," he says "be part of a small group working on a hard problem."

**No filler transitions:** No "As we've discussed," "Moving on." He transitions by introducing a new idea or question.

**No lengthy anecdotes:** He uses observation and pattern-matching, not "let me tell you about my friend Dave." When he does use anecdotes, they're compressed to a sentence or two.

**No preemptive apologies:** He states controversial ideas without softening the blow first.

**No conventional essay structure:** No "Introduction, Body, Conclusion." Sections are labeled with evocative single words. The structure follows the argument's logic, not a template.

**No extended conceits:** Analogies land and move on. He doesn't sustain a metaphor across paragraphs. Most analogies get one sentence.

**No straw men:** Even when disagreeing, he uses real examples (a Harvard brochure, actual historical cases) rather than caricatures.

**No moralizing:** He positions himself as a curious observer, not a crusader. He models independent thinking rather than directing the reader toward conclusions.

---

## Meta-Pattern: The Voice

The cumulative effect is a distinctive voice: a smart person thinking aloud, reasoning in real time, who has thought carefully about each claim and is now explaining clearly. Neither lecturing nor performing humility -- reasoning out loud with the reader as a peer.

The writing feels conversational because it follows the rhythms of thought: assertion, qualification, example, restatement, implication. It feels authoritative because it's compressed and confident. It feels honest because it acknowledges uncertainty where it exists.

PG once wrote that "style is doing things in a distinctive way without trying to." His own style exemplifies this -- the patterns are consistent enough to be identifiable but never feel like affectation.
