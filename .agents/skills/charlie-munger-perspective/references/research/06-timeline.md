# Charlie Munger: Comprehensive Life & Career Timeline

## Overview

Charles Thomas Munger (1924-2023) lived through nearly a century of American history, from the Roaring Twenties aftermath to the AI age. His life arc -- from Omaha lawyer to Warren Buffett's intellectual partner -- is itself a lesson in compounding: decades of voracious reading, multidisciplinary thinking, and patient capital allocation that produced extraordinary results late in life.

---

## I. Early Life & Education (1924-1948)

### 1924 -- Born in Omaha, Nebraska

- **Date:** January 1, 1924
- **Event:** Charles Thomas Munger born to Alfred Case Munger and Florence Munger (née Russell) in Omaha, Nebraska.
- **Context:** Omaha in the 1920s was a thriving Midwestern commercial hub. The Munger family had deep roots in the community. His grandfather, T.C. Munger, was a federal judge -- a figure young Charlie admired greatly.
- **Impact on thinking:** Growing up in Omaha gave Munger a pragmatic, no-nonsense Midwestern sensibility. The city's values of thrift, hard work, and common sense would become hallmarks of his philosophy.

> *"I was raised in Omaha, and I got a lot of my values from the people I knew there."*

### Late 1920s-1930s -- Childhood in Omaha

- **Date:** 1924-1941
- **Event:** Munger grows up in a middle-class family during the Great Depression. His father, Alfred, was a lawyer who practiced in Omaha.
- **Context:** The Depression left a permanent mark on Munger's generation. He witnessed financial ruin, bank failures, and the fragility of economic systems firsthand. His father's law practice provided stability, but the surrounding hardship was formative.
- **Impact on thinking:** Munger developed an acute awareness of financial risk, the dangers of leverage, and the importance of margin of safety. The Depression taught him that prosperity is never guaranteed.

> *"My grandfather was a federal judge. My father was a lawyer. I was expected to be a professional of some kind. The Depression showed me what happens when things go wrong."*

### 1941-1942 -- University of Michigan

- **Date:** 1941-1942
- **Event:** Munger enrolls at the University of Michigan to study mathematics.
- **Context:** World War II was raging in Europe and the Pacific. Munger, like many young men of his era, would soon have his education interrupted by military service. His time at Michigan exposed him to rigorous mathematical thinking.
- **Impact on thinking:** Mathematics gave Munger a foundation in quantitative reasoning and probabilistic thinking. He would later emphasize the importance of mental models from mathematics -- compound interest, permutations, Bayesian updating.

> *"I've always been interested in mathematics. It's the language of nature, and it teaches you to think precisely."*

### 1942-1945 -- Military Service (U.S. Army Air Corps)

- **Date:** 1942-1945
- **Event:** Munger enlists in the U.S. Army Air Corps during World War II. He serves as a meteorologist, stationed at various bases including in the Pacific theater.
- **Context:** The Army sent Munger to Caltech for meteorology training -- one of several universities the military used to quickly train officers in technical subjects. This was his first exposure to the intellectual culture of Southern California.
- **Impact on thinking:** Military service taught Munger discipline, systems thinking, and the practical application of scientific knowledge. Meteorology introduced him to probabilistic forecasting -- predicting outcomes under uncertainty, a skill directly applicable to investing.

> *"The Army taught me to work hard and to think about probabilities. In meteorology, you learn that you can't predict the weather perfectly, but you can make useful estimates."*

### 1945-1948 -- Post-War Education

- **Date:** 1945-1948
- **Event:** After the war, Munger returns to education. He attends Harvard Law School without having completed an undergraduate degree -- Harvard accepted him based on his Caltech training and military service.
- **Context:** The GI Bill opened doors for millions of returning veterans. Harvard Law in the late 1940s was a crucible of legal talent. Munger thrived in this intellectually demanding environment.
- **Impact on thinking:** Legal training gave Munger his framework for argumentation, evidence evaluation, and adversarial thinking. He learned to see issues from multiple sides -- a habit that would later make him an extraordinary investor.

> *"I went to law school because I thought it would be useful. I was right. Law teaches you to think clearly and to see both sides of an argument."*

### 1948 -- Harvard Law School Graduation

- **Date:** 1948
- **Event:** Munger graduates magna cum laude from Harvard Law School with a J.D.
- **Context:** Graduating near the top of his class from Harvard Law opened doors to prestigious law firms. Munger's academic excellence demonstrated the intellectual rigor that would later characterize his investment approach.
- **Impact on thinking:** Harvard Law reinforced Munger's belief in intellectual meritocracy and the power of rigorous analysis. He would later credit his legal training as essential to his success as an investor.

> *"Harvard Law was a wonderful experience. It taught me to think about problems in a structured way, to look for evidence, and to avoid sloppy reasoning."*

## II. Law Career (1948-1965)

### 1948-1953 -- Early Legal Practice in Los Angeles

- **Date:** 1948-1953
- **Event:** Munger returns to Southern California and begins practicing law in Los Angeles. He joins a law firm and begins building his legal career.
- **Context:** Post-war Los Angeles was booming. The aerospace, entertainment, and real estate industries were creating enormous wealth. Munger was positioned at the intersection of law and commerce.
- **Impact on thinking:** Practicing law in LA exposed Munger to business deals, corporate governance, and the mechanics of wealth creation. He began to see that law was a means to an end, not an end in itself.

### 1953 -- Divorce and Personal Tragedy

- **Date:** 1953
- **Event:** Munger's first marriage to Nancy Huggins ends in divorce. Around the same time, his son Teddy is diagnosed with leukemia.
- **Context:** The 1950s were a difficult period personally for Munger. Divorce carried social stigma, and childhood leukemia was almost always fatal in that era. Teddy Munger died in 1955 at age nine.
- **Impact on thinking:** These personal tragedies deepened Munger's stoicism and acceptance of life's harsh realities. He developed a philosophical resilience that would later help him endure market downturns and personal setbacks with equanimity.

> *"I've had my share of tragedy. It teaches you that life is not fair, and that you have to make the best of what you have."*

### 1956-1962 -- Building a Law Practice and Investing on the Side

- **Date:** 1956-1962
- **Event:** Munger continues practicing law while beginning to invest his own money. He meets various business figures in Los Angeles and begins to see the limitations of a law career for wealth creation.
- **Context:** The post-war economic boom was creating enormous opportunities in real estate, manufacturing, and finance. Munger's legal work gave him insider access to deals, but he noticed that the clients were making far more money than the lawyers.
- **Impact on thinking:** Munger began to develop his famous insight about the power of incentives. Lawyers were paid by the hour, while business owners captured the upside. This realization would eventually lead him to shift from law to investing.

> *"I realized that the clients were making the money, not the lawyers. I wanted to be on the other side of the table."*

### 1959 -- First Meeting with Warren Buffett

- **Date:** 1959
- **Event:** Munger meets Warren Buffett at a dinner party in Omaha, hosted by mutual friend Eddie Davis. The two hit it off immediately, recognizing kindred intellectual spirits.
- **Context:** Buffett was already running his investment partnership and had heard about Munger from mutual acquaintances. The meeting was arranged by Dr. Edwin Davis, who told Buffett, "You'll either love him or hate him." They talked for hours.
- **Impact on thinking:** This meeting would prove to be one of the most consequential in American business history. Buffett found an intellectual equal who shared his value-oriented approach but brought a broader multidisciplinary perspective.

> *"When I met Charlie, I knew I had met someone very special. We thought alike about almost everything. It was like finding a long-lost brother."* -- Warren Buffett

> *"I met Warren and we talked about everything. We agreed on almost nothing, but we enjoyed the conversation immensely."* -- Munger (characteristically understated)

### 1960-1962 -- Real Estate Ventures

- **Date:** 1960-1962
- **Event:** Munger begins making real estate investments in Southern California, using his legal knowledge to identify undervalued properties.
- **Context:** The LA real estate market was growing rapidly. Munger's legal training gave him an edge in understanding zoning, title issues, and contract structures. He made several successful investments that provided capital for his later investment career.
- **Impact on thinking:** Real estate taught Munger about leverage, cash flow, and the importance of buying below intrinsic value. These lessons would directly inform his approach to stock market investing.

### 1962 -- Founding of Munger, Tolles & Olson

- **Date:** 1962
- **Event:** Munger co-founds the law firm Munger, Tolles & Olson (originally Munger, Tolles, Hills & Rickershauser) with Ronald Olson, Robert Hills, and others.
- **Context:** The firm was established to provide high-quality legal services to businesses in Los Angeles. It would become one of the most prestigious law firms in California, known for its intellectual rigor and partner-driven culture.
- **Impact on thinking:** Founding a law firm taught Munger about organizational design, partner selection, and the importance of culture in institutions. He learned to value integrity and competence over credentials.

> *"We built the firm on the principle that we would hire the best people and treat them well. It worked."*

### 1962-1965 -- Transition from Law to Investment

- **Date:** 1962-1965
- **Event:** Munger gradually shifts his focus from law to investment, encouraged by his growing friendship with Buffett and his own successful investment track record.
- **Context:** Buffett was consistently outperforming the market with his partnership, and Munger was increasingly drawn to the intellectual challenge and financial rewards of investing. He continued to practice law but devoted more time to analyzing businesses.
- **Impact on thinking:** The transition period forced Munger to confront his own career path. He realized that his true calling was not law but the application of multidisciplinary thinking to capital allocation.

## III. Investment Career Begins (1962-1975)

### 1962 -- Wheeler, Munger & Co. Founded

- **Date:** 1962
- **Event:** Munger founds Wheeler, Munger & Company, an investment partnership, with Jack Wheeler.
- **Context:** The partnership was modeled on Buffett's investment partnership. Wheeler handled the administrative side, while Munger made the investment decisions. The partnership started with modest capital but grew as Munger's returns became evident.
- **Impact on thinking:** Running an investment partnership gave Munger his first experience as a fiduciary. He was now responsible for other people's money, which sharpened his discipline and risk awareness.

### 1962-1975 -- Wheeler, Munger & Co. Performance

- **Date:** 1962-1975
- **Event:** The partnership achieves strong returns but also experiences significant volatility, including a brutal bear market in 1973-1974.
- **Context:** Munger's approach was more concentrated and more willing to use leverage than Buffett's. This produced higher returns in good years but larger losses in bad years. The 1973-74 bear market was particularly painful.
- **Impact on thinking:** The volatile performance taught Munger the importance of margin of safety and the dangers of concentration and leverage. He later acknowledged that he made mistakes during this period that shaped his more conservative approach in later years.

> *"I've been through some terrible things in my life, some of which actually happened."* (Munger's favorite Mark Twain quote, often applied to market downturns)

### 1973-1974 -- Bear Market and Lessons Learned

- **Date:** 1973-1974
- **Event:** The stock market experiences a severe bear market, with the Dow Jones Industrial Average falling nearly 45%. Wheeler, Munger & Co. suffers significant losses.
- **Context:** The bear market was caused by the oil crisis, Watergate, and stagflation. Many investors, including Munger, were caught off guard by the severity and duration of the decline.
- **Impact on thinking:** This experience reinforced Munger's belief in the importance of preparing for the worst. He developed a more robust approach to risk management and became more cautious about leverage.

> *"The big money is not in the buying and selling, but in the waiting."*

### 1975 -- Wheeler, Munger & Co. Wound Down

- **Date:** 1975
- **Event:** The partnership is wound down, with investors receiving their capital back.
- **Context:** After the bear market experience, Munger decided to focus on a different approach. He was increasingly involved with Wesco Financial and his partnership with Buffett.
- **Impact on thinking:** The decision to wind down the partnership showed Munger's intellectual honesty. He recognized that his approach needed refinement and was willing to change course.

## IV. Wesco Financial and Berkshire Partnership (1978-2000)

### 1972 -- See's Candies Acquisition

- **Date:** 1972
- **Event:** Berkshire Hathaway, with Munger's strong encouragement, acquires See's Candies for $25 million.
- **Context:** See's was a West Coast candy company with strong brand loyalty and pricing power. Munger, who knew the company from his LA days, argued that the premium brand justified paying a higher price than Buffett was initially comfortable with.
- **Impact on thinking:** See's Candies became the archetype for Munger's philosophy of buying wonderful businesses at fair prices. It demonstrated the power of brand loyalty, pricing power, and the economics of quality.

> *"See's taught us that you could raise prices every year and not lose customers. That was a very important lesson."*

### 1978 -- Becomes Vice Chairman of Berkshire Hathaway

- **Date:** 1978
- **Event:** Munger officially becomes Vice Chairman of Berkshire Hathaway, formalizing his partnership with Buffett.
- **Context:** The Berkshire-Munger partnership had been developing for nearly two decades. Munger's role was not merely advisory -- he was a full intellectual partner who challenged Buffett's thinking and expanded his framework.
- **Impact on thinking:** The vice chairman role gave Munger a platform to influence Berkshire's strategy. He pushed Buffett away from "cigar butt" investing (buying mediocre businesses at very cheap prices) toward buying wonderful businesses at fair prices.

### 1970s-1980s -- Influence on Buffett's Investment Philosophy

- **Date:** 1970s-1980s
- **Event:** Munger gradually shifts Buffett's investment philosophy from Benjamin Graham's "cigar butt" approach to buying high-quality businesses with durable competitive advantages.
- **Context:** Buffett has openly credited Munger with transforming his investment approach. While Graham taught Buffett to buy cheap stocks, Munger taught him to buy great businesses.
- **Impact on thinking:** This intellectual evolution is one of the most significant in investment history. Munger introduced the concept of "moats" -- sustainable competitive advantages that protect a business from competition.

> *"Forget what you know about buying fair businesses at wonderful prices; instead, buy wonderful businesses at fair prices."* -- Warren Buffett, crediting Munger

### 1984 -- Salomon Brothers Crisis

- **Date:** 1984-1991
- **Event:** Berkshire Hathaway becomes the largest shareholder of Salomon Brothers. In 1991, a Treasury bond trading scandal threatens to destroy the firm.
- **Context:** Munger and Buffett had to step in to manage the crisis. Munger's legal background was crucial in navigating the regulatory and legal challenges. The crisis tested their leadership and ethical standards.
- **Impact on thinking:** The Salomon crisis reinforced Munger's belief in the importance of integrity and ethical behavior in business. He saw firsthand how a culture of greed and dishonesty could destroy even a successful firm.

> *"In the end, honesty is the best policy. If you have to choose between honesty and profit, choose honesty."*

### 1985 -- Wesco Financial

- **Date:** 1985-2011
- **Event:** Munger serves as Chairman of Wesco Financial, a Berkshire subsidiary that he had been involved with since the early 1970s.
- **Context:** Wesco was originally a savings and loan that Munger and Buffett acquired control of. Over time, it became a diversified holding company with interests in insurance, real estate, and furniture.
- **Impact on thinking:** Running Wesco gave Munger direct operational experience. He learned about the challenges of managing businesses, not just investing in them.

### 1986 -- Daily Journal Corporation

- **Date:** 1986
- **Event:** Munger becomes Chairman of the Daily Journal Corporation, a legal newspaper publisher based in Los Angeles.
- **Context:** The Daily Journal was a modest business, but under Munger's leadership, it would become a vehicle for his investment ideas. The company's annual meetings became famous for Munger's wide-ranging commentary.
- **Impact on thinking:** The Daily Journal gave Munger another platform for public speaking and intellectual influence. His annual meetings at the Daily Journal became legendary for their wit, wisdom, and candor.

### 1988 -- Coca-Cola Investment

- **Date:** 1988
- **Event:** Berkshire Hathaway makes its famous investment in Coca-Cola, buying $1.3 billion worth of stock.
- **Context:** Munger was a strong advocate for the Coca-Cola investment. He saw the company as the quintessential "wonderful business" -- a global brand with pricing power, distribution advantages, and cultural significance.
- **Impact on thinking:** Coca-Cola exemplified Munger's philosophy of buying businesses with strong moats. The investment would become one of Berkshire's most successful, validating the "wonderful business at a fair price" approach.

> *"Coca-Cola is the most powerful brand in the world. It's a business that will be around for a hundred years."*

### 1990s -- Expanding the Circle of Competence

- **Date:** 1990s
- **Event:** Munger continues to expand his intellectual interests, studying psychology, biology, physics, and history. He begins formalizing his "latticework of mental models" approach.
- **Context:** The 1990s were a period of intellectual growth for Munger. He was reading voraciously across multiple disciplines and synthesizing ideas into a coherent framework for decision-making.
- **Impact on thinking:** This period produced Munger's most influential ideas about multidisciplinary thinking. He argued that the best decisions come from drawing on insights from multiple fields, not just finance or economics.

> *"You've got to have models in your head. And you've got to array your experience -- both vicarious and direct -- on this latticework of models."*

### 1995 -- Harvard School Commencement Speech

- **Date:** June 1995
- **Event:** Munger delivers a famous speech at the Harvard School (now Harvard-Westlake School) titled "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment."
- **Context:** In this speech, Munger outlined 25 psychological tendencies that lead to poor decision-making. It was one of his most comprehensive presentations on human psychology and cognitive biases.
- **Impact on thinking:** This speech became one of Munger's most widely cited works. It demonstrated his belief that understanding human psychology is essential for making good decisions in business and life.

> *"The psychology of misjudgment is a terribly important subject. If you don't understand it, you're like a one-legged man in an ass-kicking contest."*

### 1998 -- Berkshire Annual Meeting Wisdom

- **Date:** May 1998
- **Event:** At the Berkshire Hathaway annual meeting, Munger delivers memorable commentary on the dot-com bubble, irrational exuberance, and the dangers of speculative mania.
- **Context:** The late 1990s dot-com bubble was inflating rapidly. Munger and Buffett were criticized for avoiding technology stocks, but they maintained their discipline.
- **Impact on thinking:** Munger's skepticism of the dot-com bubble demonstrated his commitment to circle of competence and his willingness to be contrarian. He would be vindicated when the bubble burst in 2000.

> *"If you're not willing to react with equanimity to a market price decline of 50% two or three times a century, you're not fit to be a common shareholder and you deserve the mediocre result you're going to get."*

## V. Later Years (2000-2023)

### 2000-2002 -- Dot-Com Bust Vindication

- **Date:** 2000-2002
- **Event:** The dot-com bubble bursts, destroying trillions of dollars in market value. Berkshire Hathaway's value-oriented approach is vindicated.
- **Context:** While many investors lost fortunes in speculative technology stocks, Berkshire's portfolio of traditional businesses held up relatively well. Munger and Buffett's discipline during the bubble years paid off.
- **Impact on thinking:** The dot-com bust validated Munger's emphasis on valuation, margin of safety, and circle of competence. It reinforced his belief that speculative manias always end badly.

> *"The idea that everyone can be rich by everyone buying pieces of paper from each other is absurd."*

### 2003 -- Costco Investment

- **Date:** 2003
- **Event:** Berkshire Hathaway invests in Costco, the membership warehouse retailer. Munger becomes a director of Costco.
- **Context:** Munger admired Costco's business model -- low margins, high volume, membership fees, and a culture of treating employees and customers well. He saw it as a business with a durable competitive advantage.
- **Impact on thinking:** Costco became one of Munger's favorite businesses. He frequently cited it as an example of how a company could succeed by treating all stakeholders fairly.

> *"Costco is one of the most admirable companies in the world. They do everything right."*

### 2005 -- "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment" Published

- **Date:** 2005
- **Event:** Munger's essay "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment" is published in "Poor Charlie's Almanack," a compilation of his speeches and writings edited by Peter Kaufman.
- **Context:** The book became a cult classic among investors and business thinkers. It collected Munger's most important ideas on mental models, psychology, and decision-making.
- **Impact on thinking:** "Poor Charlie's Almanack" became Munger's intellectual legacy in print. It introduced his ideas to a wider audience and cemented his reputation as one of the great thinkers of his era.

### 2007-2008 -- Financial Crisis

- **Date:** 2007-2008
- **Event:** The global financial crisis strikes, caused by the collapse of the housing bubble and the proliferation of complex financial instruments like collateralized debt obligations (CDOs).
- **Context:** Munger had warned about the dangers of excessive leverage and complex financial instruments. The crisis validated many of his concerns about Wall Street's culture of greed and short-term thinking.
- **Impact on thinking:** The financial crisis reinforced Munger's belief in the importance of simplicity, transparency, and ethical behavior in finance. He was vocal in his criticism of the financial industry's role in creating the crisis.

> *"Wall Street has gone absolutely berserk. The whole culture of Wall Street has gone crazy."*

### 2009 -- Daily Journal Annual Meeting Commentary

- **Date:** 2009
- **Event:** At the Daily Journal annual meeting, Munger delivers sharp commentary on the financial crisis, government bailouts, and the need for financial reform.
- **Context:** The financial crisis had shaken public confidence in institutions. Munger's commentary was characteristically blunt and wide-ranging, covering everything from derivatives regulation to the failures of corporate governance.
- **Impact on thinking:** Munger's willingness to criticize powerful institutions demonstrated his intellectual independence. He was not afraid to challenge conventional wisdom or speak uncomfortable truths.

> *"I think we're in a mess. And I think the mess is partly caused by the fact that the whole financial community went crazy."*

### 2010-2015 -- Daily Journal Meetings Become Cult Events

- **Date:** 2010-2015
- **Event:** The Daily Journal Corporation annual meetings in Los Angeles become must-attend events for investors, with hundreds of people packing the small venue to hear Munger's commentary.
- **Context:** As Munger's fame grew, his Daily Journal meetings became more popular. Unlike the Berkshire Hathaway meetings, which were large-scale productions, the Daily Journal meetings were intimate and allowed for more candid conversation.
- **Impact on thinking:** The Daily Journal meetings became Munger's primary platform for public commentary. He used them to share his views on everything from investing to education to public policy.

> *"I'm not sure I'd recommend my approach to anyone else. But it's worked for me."*

### 2011 -- Wesco Financial Merged into Berkshire

- **Date:** 2011
- **Event:** Wesco Financial is fully merged into Berkshire Hathaway, ending its existence as a separate entity.
- **Context:** The merger was a natural consolidation, as Berkshire already owned 80% of Wesco. Munger stepped down as chairman of Wesco but remained vice chairman of Berkshire.
- **Impact on thinking:** The merger symbolized the end of an era for Munger. Wesco had been his personal vehicle for decades, and its merger into Berkshire represented the ultimate integration of his work with Buffett's.

### 2013-2015 -- Alibaba and Chinese Investments

- **Date:** 2013-2015
- **Event:** Munger expresses admiration for Chinese companies like Alibaba and BYD, which Berkshire had invested in through its subsidiary MidAmerican Energy.
- **Context:** Munger had long been interested in Chinese business and culture. He saw parallels between China's economic rise and America's industrialization in the 19th century.
- **Impact on thinking:** Munger's interest in China demonstrated his global perspective and his willingness to invest outside his home country. He saw China as a major economic force that would shape the 21st century.

> *"China is a great country. They've done a remarkable job of lifting hundreds of millions of people out of poverty."*

### 2015-2020 -- Controversial Statements

- **Date:** 2015-2020
- **Event:** Munger makes several controversial public statements on topics including cryptocurrency, Robinhood traders, and political issues.
- **Context:** As Munger became more outspoken in his later years, some of his comments attracted criticism. His dismissal of cryptocurrency as "rat poison" and his criticism of retail investors drew both praise and backlash.
- **Impact on thinking:** Munger's controversial statements reflected his willingness to speak his mind regardless of popular opinion. While some found his comments offensive, others admired his intellectual honesty.

> *"I think the whole damn development [cryptocurrency] is disgusting and contrary to the interests of civilization."*

### 2020 -- COVID-19 Pandemic

- **Date:** 2020
- **Event:** Munger navigates the COVID-19 pandemic, attending virtual meetings and continuing to share his views on the economy and markets.
- **Context:** The pandemic was an unprecedented event that tested businesses and investors worldwide. Munger's experience through multiple crises gave him perspective on the long-term implications.
- **Impact on thinking:** Munger's response to the pandemic demonstrated his long-term perspective. While others panicked, he maintained his composure and focused on the underlying fundamentals of businesses.

> *"The best thing to do is to keep your head and not panic."*

### 2021 -- Daily Journal Annual Meeting (Virtual)

- **Date:** February 2021
- **Event:** Munger holds the Daily Journal annual meeting virtually due to COVID-19 restrictions. He delivers wide-ranging commentary on the market, meme stocks, and the dangers of speculation.
- **Context:** The GameStop short squeeze and meme stock phenomenon had captivated the financial world. Munger was characteristically blunt in his criticism of speculative trading.
- **Impact on thinking:** Munger's commentary on meme stocks demonstrated his continued relevance and his ability to apply timeless principles to new phenomena.

> *"It's really stupid to have a culture which encourages as much gambling in stocks by people who have the mindset of racetrack bettors."*

### 2022 -- Daily Journal Annual Meeting (In-Person Return)

- **Date:** February 2022
- **Event:** The Daily Journal annual meeting returns to an in-person format in Los Angeles. Munger, now 98 years old, delivers his characteristic wide-ranging commentary.
- **Context:** Despite his advanced age, Munger remained sharp and engaged. His comments covered everything from cryptocurrency to Chinese stocks to the importance of multidisciplinary thinking.
- **Impact on thinking:** Munger's continued intellectual vitality at age 98 was itself a testament to his philosophy of lifelong learning. He demonstrated that age is no barrier to clear thinking.

> *"I'm not going to change my approach at age 98. It's worked for me, and I see no reason to change."*

### 2023 -- Final Daily Journal Meeting

- **Date:** February 2023
- **Event:** Munger presides over his final Daily Journal annual meeting at age 99. He delivers a wide-ranging commentary on AI, the economy, and the importance of patience in investing.
- **Context:** This would be Munger's last major public appearance. Despite his advanced age, he remained intellectually sharp and engaged with current events.
- **Impact on thinking:** The final meeting demonstrated Munger's lifelong commitment to learning and sharing knowledge. He continued to refine his ideas until the very end.

> *"I think AI is very important. It's going to change a lot of things. But I don't think it's going to replace the need for human judgment."*

### May 2023 -- Berkshire Hathaway Annual Meeting

- **Date:** May 2023
- **Event:** Munger attends what would be his final Berkshire Hathaway annual meeting in Omaha. He sits alongside Buffett for the last time, answering questions from shareholders.
- **Context:** The meeting was emotional for those who knew it might be Munger's last. Buffett and Munger reflected on their 60+ year partnership and the lessons they had learned together.
- **Impact on thinking:** The final Berkshire meeting was a celebration of one of the most successful partnerships in business history. It demonstrated the power of intellectual collaboration and shared values.

> *"We've had a wonderful time together. I wouldn't trade it for anything."* -- Munger at his final Berkshire meeting

## VI. Passing and Legacy (November 2023)

### November 28, 2023 -- Charlie Munger Passes Away

- **Date:** November 28, 2023
- **Event:** Charles Thomas Munger dies at age 99 at a hospital in Los Angeles, just weeks before his 100th birthday.
- **Context:** Munger's passing marked the end of an era in American business. He had been Warren Buffett's intellectual partner for over 60 years and had helped build Berkshire Hathaway into one of the most successful companies in history.
- **Impact on thinking:** Munger's death prompted an outpouring of tributes from across the business and investment world. His legacy as a thinker, investor, and teacher was universally acknowledged.

### Tributes and Legacy

- **Date:** November-December 2023
- **Event:** Tributes pour in from across the business world. Warren Buffett issues a heartfelt statement. Media outlets publish extensive retrospectives on Munger's life and career.
- **Context:** The tributes reflected the breadth of Munger's influence. He was remembered not just as an investor, but as a teacher, philosopher, and role model for clear thinking.
- **Impact on thinking:** Munger's legacy is not just financial -- it's intellectual. His ideas about mental models, multidisciplinary thinking, and the psychology of misjudgment will continue to influence decision-makers for generations.

> *"Charlie Munger was the best partner anyone could ever have. His wisdom, humor, and integrity made me a better person."* -- Warren Buffett

> *"Charlie's greatest contribution was not to finance, but to the art of thinking. He showed us how to think clearly about complex problems."*

## VII. Key Quotes by Period -- Evolution of Thinking

### Early Career (1960s-1970s)

> *"The big money is not in the buying and selling, but in the waiting."*

> *"I've been through some terrible things in my life, some of which actually happened."*

> *"Invert, always invert. Turn a situation or problem upside down. What happens if all our plans go wrong?"*

### Middle Years (1980s-1990s)

> *"Spend each day trying to be a little wiser than you were when you woke up. Discharge your duties faithfully and well. Slug it out one inch at a time, day by day. At the end of the day -- if you live long enough -- most people get what they deserve."*

> *"The best thing a human being can do is to help another human being know more."*

> *"You don't have to be brilliant, only a little bit wiser than the other guys, on average, for a long, long time."*

> *"Acknowledging what you don't know is the dawning of wisdom."*

### Later Years (2000s-2020s)

> *"The world is not driven by greed. It's driven by envy."*

> *"I constantly see people rise in life who are not the smartest, sometimes not even the most diligent, but they are learning machines. They go to bed every night a little wiser than they were when they got up."*

> *"Knowing what you don't know is more useful than being brilliant."*

> *"You should avoid sloth and unreliability."*

> *"I try to get rid of people who always confidently answer questions about which they don't have any real knowledge."*

### Final Years (2020s)

> *"I think the world is full of foolish gamblers, and they will not do as well as the patient investor."*

> *"It's remarkable how much long-term advantage people like us have gotten by trying to be consistently not stupid, instead of trying to be very intelligent."*

> *"I'm not entitled to have an opinion unless I can state the arguments against my position better than the people who are in opposition."*
