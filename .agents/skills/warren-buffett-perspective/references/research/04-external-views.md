## 1. Charlie Munger's Evaluation

### On Buffett's Strengths

**Learning ability** [HIGH]:
> "Warren is one of the best learning machines on this earth. The turtle who makes the most progress is the one who sticks his neck out."

Source: Multiple Berkshire Hathaway annual meeting transcripts; widely cited in biographical accounts.

**Intellectual honesty** [HIGH]:
Munger consistently praised Buffett's ability to avoid common psychological biases and think rationally. He credited Buffett's "circle of competence" -- staying within areas he truly understood.

**The evolution from cigar-butts to quality** [HIGH]:
Munger helped shift Buffett from buying "fair businesses at wonderful prices" to buying "wonderful businesses at fair prices." Buffett himself acknowledged this:
> "It took Charlie Munger to get me off the cigar-butt approach."
> "Boy, if I had listened only to Ben [Graham] and not also to Charlie Munger, would I ever be a lot poorer."

Source: Berkshire Hathaway letters; Wikipedia (Benjamin Graham article).

### On Buffett's Weaknesses (Said with Humor)

**Early investment style** [HIGH]:
Munger quipped that Buffett started out as a "cigar-butt investor" who was "looking for dregs" -- referring to the Ben Graham-inspired deep value approach that Munger helped evolve beyond.

### On Their Partnership

**Division of labor** [HIGH]:
Munger was the "architect" of ideas; Buffett was the "dealmaker." Buffett described Munger as his "closest partner and right-hand man" and called him the "architect" of modern Berkshire Hathaway's philosophy.

**Munger's humility** [HIGH]:
> "I think I've been a good assistant to Warren."

He famously said at annual meetings: "I have nothing to add" -- then often followed it with sharp insights.

**Buffett on Munger** [HIGH]:
> "Charlie has the best 30-second mind in the world. He goes from A to Z in one move."
> "Charlie never sought to take credit for his role as creator but instead let me take the bows."
> "Berkshire Hathaway could not have been built without Charlie's inspiration, wisdom, and participation."

Source: Berkshire Hathaway annual letters; Buffett's tribute after Munger's death (November 28, 2023).

### On Avoiding Stupidity

**Shared philosophy** [HIGH]:
> "We're not saying we're better than everyone else, we're just saying we avoid the standard stupidities."

Munger in his final 2023 CNBC interview credited a "long-held sense of caution" and his ability "to avoid all standard ways of failing."

Source: Wikipedia (Charlie Munger article); CNBC interview.

## 2. Other Investor Views

### Bill Gates

**Close friendship** [HIGH]:
Gates served on the Berkshire Hathaway board until March 2020. They co-founded The Giving Pledge in 2010. They collaborated on TerraPower (nuclear energy) with Buffett's PacifiCorp.

**Philosophical alignment** [HIGH]:
Both committed to giving away the vast majority of their wealth. Gates stated his children "will inherit less than 1% of his wealth."

**On Buffett's letters** [MEDIUM]:
Gates has frequently described Buffett's annual shareholder letters as among the best things he reads every year, praising Buffett's ability to explain complex financial concepts simply.

Source: Wikipedia (Bill Gates, Berkshire Hathaway articles).

### Bill Ackman

**Admiration for capital allocation** [HIGH]:
Ackman "values investment stability in a style of Warren Buffett," reflected in his pursuit of "permanent capital" through holdings like Howard Hughes Holdings.

**Investment philosophy overlap** [HIGH]:
Ackman's approach combines bold contrarianism with long-term stability thinking inspired by Buffett. He has stated his "most successful investments have always been controversial."

Source: Wikipedia (Bill Ackman article).

### Howard Marks (Oaktree Capital)

**Mutual respect with Buffett** [HIGH]:
Buffett said: "When I see memos from Howard Marks in my mail, they're the first thing I open and read." Buffett added that he always learns from both Marks' memos and his books.

**Shared philosophy elements** [HIGH]:
Both believe in being "fearful when others are greedy." Marks focuses on risk management and steady returns over spectacular gains. His goal is a "high batting average" over "home runs."

**Marks' approach** [HIGH]:
Marks believes gaining an edge through research alone is difficult because "so many smart people are doing it already," so advantages come from better interpretation of data, managing investment psychology, and understanding market cycles.

Source: Wikipedia (Howard Marks article).

### Ray Dalio (Bridgewater)

**Different approach, shared principles** [MEDIUM]:
Dalio runs a "global macro firm" focused on currency and fixed income markets, using "risk parity" strategy. He popularized the "D-Process" concept for deleveraging. While Dalio has praised Buffett's long-term compounding approach in interviews, the Wikipedia article on Dalio contains no direct quotes about Buffett.

**Key difference** [HIGH]:
Dalio's approach is systematic and macro-driven vs. Buffett's concentrated fundamental stock picking. Dalio's Pure Alpha strategy seeks "excess returns a money manager can create in addition to market returns when adjusted for risk."

Source: Wikipedia (Ray Dalio article).

### Mohnish Pabrai

**Devoted follower** [HIGH]:
Pabrai "has high regard for Warren Buffett and has said that his investment style is 'cloned' from Buffett and other value investors." His hedge funds were "inspired by Buffett Partnerships."

**The cloning philosophy** [HIGH]:
In *The Dhandho Investor* and *Mosaic: Perspectives on Investing*, Pabrai distilled "the Warren Buffett method of investing down to a few points." His approach centers on adopting proven strategies rather than developing original ones.

**Personal connection** [HIGH]:
In June 2007, Pabrai and Guy Spier bid $650,100 for a charity lunch with Buffett. Pabrai described this as his "guru dakshina" -- a traditional offering to a teacher.

Source: Wikipedia (Mohnish Pabrai article).

### Todd Combs and Ted Weschler

**Buffett's chosen successors for investing** [HIGH]:
Combs was hired in 2010; the Wall Street Journal reported "Buffett had tapped Combs to eventually replace him as the chief investment officer of Berkshire Hathaway." Together, Combs and Weschler "were frequently cited as a potential successor of Warren Buffett as the chief investment officer of Berkshire."

**Combs' background** [HIGH]:
Founded Castle Point Capital, which posted a cumulative return of 34% during 2005-2010. He later served as CEO of GEICO (2020-2025) while managing portfolio duties. In January 2026, he departed to lead JPMorgan Chase's Strategic Investment Group.

Source: Wikipedia (Todd Combs article).

## 3. Criticisms

### "Too Old" / Succession Concerns

**Status** [HIGH]:
Buffett announced at the May 2025 annual meeting that Greg Abel would become CEO at year-end. Abel assumed the role in January 2026. The succession was closely held -- "only Buffett's children knew the timing of his retirement announcement" and Abel himself received no advance warning.

**The concern** [HIGH]:
Questions about succession and Buffett's age (now 95) have been ongoing topics for over a decade. Abel was chosen for his "strong work ethic and strategic thinking," "commitment to value investing," and belief he would "maintain the corporate culture established in previous decades."

Source: Wikipedia (Greg Abel article).

### "Missed Tech"

**The record** [HIGH]:
Buffett famously avoided technology stocks for decades, saying he didn't understand them. He turned down Amazon in 1994. His investment philosophy "does not like to invest in companies that may undergo significant change."

**The reversal** [HIGH]:
Buffett built a massive position in Apple starting in 2016, though he reduced the stake by nearly 50% in Q2 2024, selling $75.5 billion worth. He considers Apple a consumer products company rather than a tech stock.

**The irony** [HIGH]:
The biggest tech miss was not an investment but the original Berkshire Hathaway textile business itself. Buffett called it "the biggest investment mistake he had ever made," estimating it cost "about $200 billion" in foregone compounded returns.

Source: Wikipedia (Berkshire Hathaway, Warren Buffett articles).

### "Too Close to Wall Street During 2008"

**The Goldman Sachs deal** [HIGH]:
On September 23, 2008 -- one day after MUFG acquired 20% of Morgan Stanley and one week after Lehman's bankruptcy -- Berkshire made a $5 billion investment in Goldman Sachs. This earned roughly $1.8 billion in profit plus over $2 billion from warrants.

**The criticism** [HIGH]:
Buffett "ran into criticism... that he had allocated capital too early resulting in suboptimal deals." Berkshire suffered a 77% earnings drop in Q3 2008, and "several of his later deals suffered large mark-to-market losses."

**The defense** [HIGH]:
The investments were structured as preferred stock with favorable terms (10% dividend on Goldman preferred), giving Berkshire downside protection. The deals were widely seen as Buffett deploying capital when others were afraid -- consistent with his "be greedy when others are fearful" philosophy.

Source: Wikipedia (Warren Buffett, Berkshire Hathaway, 2007-2008 financial crisis articles).

### Salomon Brothers Crisis Management

**The 1991 scandal** [HIGH]:
Trader Paul Mozer submitted "false bids in an attempt to purchase more treasury bonds than permitted by one buyer." CEO John Gutfreund resigned and was fined $100,000 and "barred from serving as a chief executive of a brokerage firm."

**Buffett's intervention** [HIGH]:
"Warren Buffett briefly stepped into the CEO and chairman position" to stabilize the firm. He promoted Deryck Maughan to lead permanently. Salomon was fined $190 million and required to establish a $100 million restitution fund.

**Assessment** [MEDIUM]:
The crisis management is generally viewed as a success -- Buffett prevented Salomon from losing its trading licenses, which would have been fatal. However, critics argue he was too slow to act and too trusting of management.

Source: Wikipedia (Salomon Brothers article); Martin Mayer's *Nightmare on Wall Street* (1993).

### "Not Enough Philanthropy Earlier"

**The timeline** [HIGH]:
Buffett co-founded The Giving Pledge in 2010 with Bill Gates. He has reportedly donated over $60 billion, making him one of the most prolific philanthropists.

**The criticism** [HIGH]:
Critics note that Buffett accumulated wealth for decades before committing to major philanthropy. A 2025 Institute for Policy Studies report found the 32 original U.S. pledgers who remain billionaires "collectively become about 166 percent wealthier since signing on in 2010."

**The Giving Pledge's limitations** [HIGH]:
- Non-binding: "a moral commitment to give, not a legal contract"
- Among 22 pledgers who had died, "just 8... actually gave away enough to meet the 'half of wealth' pledge"
- ~80% of contributions went to private foundations or donor-advised funds rather than directly to operating charities, which critics say "delays impact and preserves donor control"
- Rate of new signers has dropped sharply

**Buffett's changing approach** [HIGH]:
Buffett "became distant from the Gates Foundation following the Gates' divorce" and redirected his giving by transferring "$500 million to each of his three children's charitable foundations." He acknowledged "his original philanthropic plans were not 'feasible.'"

Source: Wikipedia (The Giving Pledge article).

### ProPublica "Sugar Daddy" Criticism

**The investigation** [MEDIUM]:
ProPublica's "Secret IRS Files" series (starting June 2021) revealed that Buffett paid a very low "true tax rate" -- estimated around 0.1% on his growing wealth from 2014-2018. His wealth largely grew through unrealized capital gains in Berkshire stock, which are not taxed until sold.

**The "sugar daddy" characterization** [MEDIUM]:
ProPublica reported on Buffett's personal financial arrangements and philanthropic connections. The criticism centered on the disconnect between his public advocacy for higher taxes on the wealthy and his personal use of legal loopholes like "buy-borrow-die" (buying assets, borrowing against them instead of selling, and passing them to heirs with a stepped-up basis).

**Munger's defense** [HIGH]:
When conflict-of-interest allegations arose, Munger dismissed them: "I don't think there's the slightest chance that Warren Buffett is doing something that is deeply evil."

Source: Wikipedia (Warren Buffett article); ProPublica "Secret IRS Files" series.

### Other Criticisms

**Estate tax conflict of interest** [HIGH]:
Critics argued Buffett has "a personal interest in the continuation of the estate tax, since Berkshire Hathaway benefited from the estate tax in past business dealings."

**SEC scrutiny** [HIGH]:
In 1974, the SEC opened a formal investigation into Buffett and Berkshire's acquisition of Wesco Financial "due to possible conflict of interest," though no charges were brought. The SEC also demanded Berkshire produce "a more robust disclosure" regarding his put options during the crisis.

**ConocoPhillips timing** [HIGH]:
Buffett himself acknowledged buying "a large amount of ConocoPhillips stock when oil and gas prices were near their peak" and that "the terrible timing of my purchase has cost Berkshire several billion dollars."

Source: Wikipedia (Warren Buffett article).

## 4. Biographer / Journalist Views

### Alice Schroeder (The Snowball, 2008)

**Access and candor** [HIGH]:
Buffett granted Schroeder extraordinary access but imposed a notable condition: he instructed her that "where accounts of his life differed, to always use the 'less flattering version.'" This suggests the book was intended to present a candid, unvarnished portrait.

**Critical framing** [HIGH]:
A CNN interview with Schroeder was headlined around the theme that Buffett's career involved a "battle of greed vs. principles," suggesting she explored internal tensions in his character.

**Reception** [HIGH]:
- Washington Post: "the most detailed glimpse inside Warren Buffett and his world that we likely will ever get...a bible for capitalists"
- Los Angeles Times: "the most authoritative portrait of one of the most important American investors of our time"
- The Economist noted the book was not the best source for "detailed analyses of his investment record," suggesting Schroeder focused more on Buffett's personal life and character

Source: Wikipedia (The Snowball article).

### Carol Loomis (Fortune)

**Unique position** [HIGH]:
Loomis was a "longtime friend of Warren Buffett's" who served as the "pro bono editor of his annual letter to shareholders." She was also a Berkshire Hathaway shareholder.

**Promoting Buffett's ideas** [HIGH]:
In 2001, she "convinced Warren Buffett to write an article in Fortune explaining the Buffett indicator." She compiled her extensive coverage into *Tap Dancing to Work: Warren Buffett on Practically Everything, 1966-2012* (2012).

**Credibility** [HIGH]:
As senior editor-at-large at Fortune for over 60 years (retiring in 2014), Loomis was one of the most respected financial journalists in American history, having won multiple Gerald Loeb Awards and the Gerald Loeb Lifetime Achievement Award.

**Potential conflict** [MEDIUM]:
Her dual role as friend/editor and journalist/shareholder raises questions about objectivity, though her decades of work are generally respected.

Source: Wikipedia (Carol Loomis article).

### Andrew Ross Sorkin

**Crisis coverage** [HIGH]:
Sorkin's *Too Big to Fail* (2009) documented the 2008 financial crisis, with Buffett appearing as a character (played by Edward Asner in the HBO adaptation). Buffett's role as an investor during the crisis was part of Sorkin's broader narrative about Wall Street's response.

Source: Wikipedia (Andrew Ross Sorkin article).

### Roger Lowenstein (Buffett: The Making of an American Capitalist, 1995)

**The biography** [HIGH]:
Lowenstein's *Buffett: The Making of an American Capitalist* (Random House, 1995) was his first book and helped establish him as a prominent financial writer. He spent over a decade reporting for The Wall Street Journal, including writing its "Heard on the Street" column.

**Context** [HIGH]:
Lowenstein's other notable works include *When Genius Failed* (2000) about LTCM's collapse and *America's Bank* (2015) about the Federal Reserve's creation. He is also a director of the Sequoia Fund.

Source: Wikipedia (Roger Lowenstein article).

## 5. Comparison with Peers

### Buffett vs. Soros (Active vs. Passive-Adjacent)

**Style contrast** [HIGH]:
- Buffett: Value investing, long-term buy-and-hold, ~20% CAGR over ~60 years
- Soros: Global macro trading, currency speculation, ~30% returns over ~30 years

**Key difference** [HIGH]:
Buffett's time horizon is decades; Soros's is weeks to months. Buffett buys businesses; Soros trades macroeconomic trends. Soros's Quantum Fund "since its inception in 1973, the fund has generated $40 billion." His famous 1992 pound short "made him a profit of $1 billion."

**Philosophical difference** [HIGH]:
Buffett believes markets are generally efficient over long periods; Soros's "reflexivity theory" holds that markets are inherently unstable and self-reinforcing.

Source: Wikipedia (George Soros article).

### Buffett vs. Simons (Quant vs. Fundamental)

**The numbers** [HIGH]:
- Buffett: ~20% CAGR over 60 years
- Simons' Medallion Fund: 66.1% average gross annual return (39.1% net) between 1988 and 2018

**The gap** [HIGH]:
Medallion generated over "$100 billion in trading profits" since inception. In 2008, it "gained 80% despite substantial fees" while most funds suffered. Simons was called "the greatest investor on Wall Street" and "the most successful hedge fund manager of all time."

**The caveat** [HIGH]:
Medallion is closed to outside investors (limited to employees). Renaissance's other funds (RIEF, RIDA) that were open to outside investors did not perform nearly as well -- RIEF lost 16% in 2008.

**Edward Witten on Simons** [HIGH]:
"It's startling to see such a highly successful mathematician achieve success in another field."

Source: Wikipedia (Jim Simons article).

### Buffett vs. Index Funds

**The bet** [HIGH]:
Buffett famously won a $1 million bet that a simple S&P 500 index fund would outperform a collection of hedge funds over a decade. He is listed among "strong proponents of index funds."

**The academic case** [HIGH]:
Research consistently shows "most active investors (stock pickers) within a given market segment underperform the relevant index after fees and taxes." Buffett himself said: "when trillions of dollars are managed by Wall Streeters charging high fees, it will usually be the managers who reap outsized profits."

**The paradox** [HIGH]:
Buffett advocates index funds for most investors but runs an actively managed portfolio himself. His 1984 speech "The Superinvesters of Graham-and-Doddsville" examined fellow Graham-Newman alumni, concluding that value investing succeeds on average over the long term. The speech argued that if long-term winners "operate independently of each other," their success "is more than a lottery win; it is a triumph of the right strategy."

**Academic dismissal** [HIGH]:
Despite Buffett's track record, academia largely ignored his 1984 paper. A 2004 search of 23,000 economics papers found only 20 references to any Buffett publication. Critics like William Sharpe dismissed Buffett as "a three-sigma event" (1 in 370). However, Seth Klarman noted that Buffett's argument had "never...been addressed by the efficient-market theorists."

Source: Wikipedia (Index fund, The Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville articles).

## 6. What Even Critics Concede

### Track Record

**The numbers** [HIGH]:
Between 1965 and 2023, Berkshire Hathaway delivered a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 19.8% in shareholder returns, versus 10.2% CAGR for the S&P 500. The stock posted negative performance in only eleven years during that entire span.

**The milestone** [HIGH]:
In August 2024, Berkshire became the first non-technology U.S. company valued at over $1 trillion.

**Notable wins** [HIGH]:
- See's Candies (1972): Acquired for $25 million; by 2019, "returned over $2 billion, or an 8000% return"
- Goldman Sachs (2008): $5 billion investment earned ~$1.8 billion profit plus $2 billion+ from warrants
- Bank of America (2011): $5 billion investment yielded ~$12 billion profit by 2017

Source: Wikipedia (Berkshire Hathaway article).

### Communication Skill

**The letters** [HIGH]:
Buffett's annual shareholder letters are among the most widely read financial documents in the world. Carol Loomis served as pro bono editor for decades. Bill Gates has described them as among the best things he reads every year.

**Plain language** [HIGH]:
Buffett is famous for explaining complex financial concepts in simple, folksy language. His quotes about gold ("It gets dug out of the ground... dig another hole, bury it again and pay people to stand around guarding it") and cigarettes ("It costs a penny to make. Sell it for a dollar. It's addictive. And there's fantastic brand loyalty") are widely cited.

Source: Wikipedia (Warren Buffett article).

### Integrity

**Munger's defense** [HIGH]:
When conflict-of-interest allegations arose, Munger dismissed them: "I don't think there's the slightest chance that Warren Buffett is doing something that is deeply evil."

**Self-criticism** [HIGH]:
Buffett has been remarkably candid about his mistakes:
- Berkshire Hathaway textile business: "a monumentally stupid decision" triggered by anger over a $0.125 per share undercut
- ConocoPhillips: "a major mistake" with "terrible timing"
- Tesco: "the delay in selling shares was costly" (lost $444 million)
- Kraft Heinz: "he overpaid in the deal"
- Precision Castparts: wrote down ~$10 billion after admitting overpayment

**The "less flattering version"** [HIGH]:
When working with biographer Alice Schroeder, Buffett instructed her that "where accounts of his life differed, to always use the 'less flattering version.'"

Source: Wikipedia (Warren Buffett, Berkshire Hathaway, The Snowball articles).

### Temperament

**The core insight** [HIGH]:
Buffett's principle of being "greedy when others are fearful, and fearful when others are greedy" is widely admired even by those who disagree with his specific investment choices.

**Lorimer Davidson's assessment** [HIGH]:
GEICO VP Lorimer Davidson found Buffett to be an "extraordinary man" after only fifteen minutes of conversation -- when Buffett was a young man knocking on GEICO's door.

Source: Wikipedia (Warren Buffett article).
