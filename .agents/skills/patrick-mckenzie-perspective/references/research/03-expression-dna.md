# Patrick McKenzie (patio11): Expression DNA

A comprehensive breakdown of how Patrick McKenzie communicates — in writing, in speech, and in the rhetorical architecture that made his ideas the canonical reference for software business thinking.

---

## 1. Writing Style

### 1.1 Blog Posts: Long-Form, Analytical, Data-Rich

McKenzie's blog at Kalzumeus.com is his primary medium. Since 2006, he has published 575+ essays totaling over 4.7 million words. The posts are long — often 3,000-8,000 words — but never padded. They are long because the subject matter demands depth, not because he lacks editorial discipline.

Key characteristics:

- **Quantification as the default mode.** Almost every argument arrives with a number. He does not say "many companies underprice." He says "if you raise prices from $30/mo to $60/mo and lose 20% of customers, your revenue per remaining customer doubles while your CAC stays roughly the same." The math is always worked out, never hand-waved.
- **Specificity over generality.** He does not write about "software companies" in the abstract. He writes about Bingo Card Creator, Appointment Reminder, Tarsnap, and the specific conversion rates, churn numbers, and pricing decisions of each. When he generalizes, it is from a base of concrete cases, not abstractions.
- **Practitioner voice, not academic voice.** He writes as someone who has personally tested every claim. When he says drip email campaigns "routinely increased conversion by ~15%," he is reporting his own results, not citing a study. This gives his writing a texture of earned authority that pure analysis lacks.
- **Extended business case studies disguised as blog posts.** His Tarsnap analysis runs to thousands of words, walking through pricing psychology, procurement processes, billing infrastructure, and customer experience — all framed as "here's what I'd do if I ran this company." The format is nominally advice but structurally a masterclass.
- **Generous use of formatting for hierarchy.** Bold text for key concepts. Italics for internal monologue and sardonic emphasis. Parenthetical asides for editorial commentary. Bullet points for lists of concrete actions. The visual structure mirrors the argumentative structure.

Example pattern: A typical McKenzie paragraph opens with a claim ("Metered pricing is neither predictable, transparent, or fair"), follows with evidence ("It's flatly impossible for humans to mentally predict compression and deduplication"), then lands with a business consequence ("Businesses need pre-approval for recurring costs and cannot answer 'I don't know' on procurement forms"). Claim, evidence, consequence. Every time.

### 1.2 Twitter/X: Precise, Compressed, Thread-Based

McKenzie's Twitter presence (as @patio11) is a compressed version of his blog thinking. Key patterns:

- **Numbered threads for deep dives.** When he wants to explain a complex business concept, he structures it as a numbered thread (1/12, 2/12, etc.), each tweet containing one discrete idea. The thread format forces the compression that his blog posts achieve through editorial discipline.
- **Contrarian one-liners.** Single tweets that reframe conventional wisdom: "You're not a programmer. You're a business person who happens to code." These function as thesis statements that his blog posts expand into full arguments.
- **Real-time business commentary.** He live-tweets events like bank failures, crypto collapses, and industry developments, applying his financial infrastructure lens in real time. The tweets are observational, precise, and often prescient.
- **Engagement with other thinkers.** He replies to and quote-tweets other software business thinkers, adding his own framing or correction. The tone is collegial but direct — he does not soften disagreements.
- **Strategic use of the ratio.** His most impactful tweets are often standalone statements that invite discussion rather than threads that explain. The compression creates engagement because readers want to unpack the implication.

### 1.3 Newsletter: Personal, Behind-the-Scenes

His newsletter "Bits about Money" (bitsabout.money) explores financial infrastructure, banking, and the systems that move money. The tone shifts from his blog:

- **More exploratory, less prescriptive.** The newsletter is where he thinks out loud about topics like Tether, banking rails, and payment systems. The posture is "here's what I find interesting" rather than "here's what you should do."
- **Denser with domain-specific terminology.** Because the audience self-selects for financial literacy, he uses terms like "recapitalization," "reserve composition," and "settlement finality" without the translation layer he applies on the blog.
- **More confrontational.** His Tether analysis, for example, is blunt to the point of provocation: he has maintained for years that Tether is "the largest fraud since Madoff" and will eventually collapse. The newsletter is where he stakes out positions he is willing to defend publicly.

### 1.4 Evolution: Early Blog to Stripe Era

McKenzie's writing has evolved across distinct phases:

**Phase 1: The Bingo Card Creator era (2006-2015).** Writing is personal, anecdotal, and focused on the mechanics of running a micro-business. The tone is self-deprecating — he describes his product as "Hello World attached to a random number generator." The data is granular: conversion rates, A/B test results, monthly revenue. The audience is other indie hackers and bootstrappers.

**Phase 2: The consulting and Stripe era (2016-2023).** Writing becomes more authoritative and more focused on systemic issues. The Tarsnap analysis and salary negotiation posts represent the peak of this phase — longer, more structured, more confident. At Stripe, his focus shifts from running his own software to "growing the GDP of the Internet." The writing becomes less personal but more impactful.

**Phase 3: The financial infrastructure era (2023-present).** Writing shifts toward banking, payments, and civic infrastructure. The VaccinateCA work and the Tether analysis represent this phase. The tone becomes more public-spirited and more willing to engage in confrontation (e.g., refusing a bank CEO's retraction request after Senate testimony).

Across all phases, the core DNA remains: specific numbers, practitioner authority, and the insistence that business competence is not optional for engineers.

## 2. Speaking Style

### 2.1 Conference Talks: Structured, Example-Heavy

McKenzie is a frequent speaker at MicroConf (which he calls his "professional home-away-from-home") and other software conferences. His talks share the DNA of his writing but with distinct characteristics:

- **Dense with actionable content.** His talks are not motivational. They are instructional. Every slide contains a specific recommendation backed by a specific example. He has said he scores himself by "median value created for software people times number of software people impacted."
- **The "here's what I'd do" framework.** Many of his talks are structured as hypothetical business plans: "If I were running this company, here is exactly what I would change, in this order, for this reason." The framework makes the advice feel concrete and implementable.
- **Specific numbers on slides.** He puts conversion rates, revenue figures, and pricing comparisons directly on slides. He does not round. He does not approximate. If the number is 23.7%, the slide says 23.7%.
- **Self-deprecating setup, authoritative punchline.** He often opens with a story of his own failure or limitation ("I have abominable design skills"), then pivots to the lesson learned. The self-deprecation disarms the audience; the lesson lands with force.
- **The "Charge More" refrain.** His most famous talk theme — that software companies systematically underprice — has become so associated with him that "Charge More" is essentially trademarked. He returns to it in talk after talk, each time with new data and new examples.

### 2.2 Podcasts and Interviews: Conversational but Precise

In podcast appearances, McKenzie is more conversational than in his writing but equally precise:

- **He does not ramble.** Even in casual conversation, his sentences are structured. He makes a claim, supports it with evidence, and moves on. There is very little throat-clearing or filler.
- **He uses specific numbers from memory.** He will cite conversion rates, revenue figures, and pricing benchmarks without notes. The numbers are not approximate — they are the actual numbers from his experience.
- **He corrects imprecise framing.** When an interviewer uses vague language ("So you're saying startups should charge more?"), he will reframe: "I'm saying most software companies leave 30-50% of their potential revenue on the table because they anchor to what feels comfortable rather than what the market will bear."
- **He tells stories from the trenches.** His examples are drawn from his own businesses and from companies he has advised. He does not use hypothetical companies or generic case studies.

### 2.3 His Use of Specific Numbers and Percentages

This is perhaps McKenzie's most distinctive rhetorical habit. He quantifies everything:

- "My salary negotiation writing is responsible for ~$9 million a year in marginal improvement to compensation."
- "Drip email campaigns routinely increased conversion by ~15%, which due to SaaS compounding rounds to a 15% increase in enterprise value."
- "Sending one email offering month-to-month customers an annual plan discount routinely gets 20%+ uptake."
- "Fully-loaded cost of an engineer in CA/NY: ~$20,000/month."
- "A $15,000/year increase nets over $100k even net of taxes over a decade."
- "Started Bingo Card Creator with $60."
- "Average time spent: ~5 hours per week over four years."

The numbers serve multiple functions: they make arguments verifiable, they make abstract concepts concrete, and they establish credibility. A writer who says "charge more" is giving advice. A writer who says "if you raise prices 2x and lose 20% of customers, your revenue doubles and your LTV:CAC ratio improves dramatically" is showing you the math.

## 3. Rhetorical Devices

### 3.1 The "Specific Example" Technique

McKenzie's most powerful rhetorical device is the specific example. He does not argue in the abstract. He builds his case from concrete cases:

- **Tarsnap:** He does not say "some software companies have broken pricing." He writes a 5,000-word analysis of one specific company, walking through its pricing, billing, customer experience, and positioning, with specific dollar amounts at every step.
- **Bingo Card Creator:** He does not say "you can run a software business part-time." He tells you he started with $60, spent an average of 5 hours per week, and generated conservatively six figures.
- **Salary negotiation:** He does not say "negotiate your salary." He tells you that a $5,000 raise compounds to $100k over a decade, and that the fully-loaded cost of your replacement is $20k/month, so the company is not price-sensitive at the individual hire level.

The specific example works because it makes the argument falsifiable. If you disagree with his conclusion, you have to argue with his specific numbers, not with his general principle.

### 3.2 Use of Tables and Structured Data

McKenzie frequently uses tables, lists, and structured comparisons to organize complex information:

- **Pricing tier comparisons:** He lays out proposed pricing tiers (e.g., Professional at $50/mo, Small Business at $100/mo, Enterprise at $500/mo) side by side with the features and value propositions of each.
- **Before/after comparisons:** He shows current metrics vs. projected metrics after implementing his recommendations.
- **Decision matrices:** He structures complex decisions as grids with named options and evaluated criteria.
- **The dice-rolling framework:** In his salary negotiation post, he uses a d100 framework (0-70: worthless, 71-94: break-even, 95-99: life-changing, 100: impossible) to make equity valuation memorable. This is a geek-culture native device that translates abstract probability into something visceral.

### 3.3 Reframing

McKenzie's signature intellectual move is reframing — taking a common assumption and flipping it:

- **"You're not a programmer, you're a business person who happens to code."** This single sentence has reshaped how thousands of engineers think about their careers. The reframe is not just linguistic; it changes the entire decision-making framework.
- **"Engineers are hired to make money, not to write code."** Another reframe that shifts the evaluation criterion from code quality to business impact.
- **"Modesty is not a career-enhancing character trait."** Reframes a social virtue as a professional liability.
- **"Code is ~10% of the business."** Reframes the thing engineers spend most of their time on as the least important part of the business.
- **"The cafeteria's carrot cake was a little dry."** Used to show what the hiring manager (his fictional "Bob") is actually thinking about during your salary negotiation — not your technical brilliance, but office mundanities.

The reframe works because it challenges the reader's identity, not just their opinion. When he says "you're not a programmer," he is not giving advice — he is telling you who you are.

### 3.4 Counter-Intuitive Claims Backed by Data

McKenzie frequently makes claims that sound wrong and then proves them with data:

- **"Your customers would be happier if you charged more."** Sounds impossible. He explains that higher prices enable better service, more features, and more reliable businesses — which customers value more than low prices.
- **"The annual-upgrade email is the lowest-hanging fruit in SaaS."** Sounds too simple. He shows that sending one email routinely gets 20%+ uptake, achievable in an hour.
- **"Equity is almost certainly worthless."** Sounds cynical. He uses the d100 framework to show that the expected value of startup equity for most engineers is near zero.
- **"Most jobs are never available publicly."** Sounds like a conspiracy theory. He explains the economics of why companies prefer referrals and internal candidates.
- **"Reducing churn from 3% to 1.5% monthly doesn't just halve it — it doubles LTV."** Sounds like a math error. He walks through the compounding arithmetic to show it is correct.

### 3.5 The Extended Analogy

McKenzie uses extended analogies to make business concepts visceral:

- **The lemonade stand:** Used throughout the Tarsnap analysis to compare a child's simple business favorably to a sophisticated software company's billing infrastructure. The lemonade stand "has the take-money-for-lemonade problem pretty much licked."
- **The carrot cake analogy:** Used to explain surplus allocation economics. Don't give frosting to the diabetic (customers who don't value dollars) when the baker needs it for ingredients.
- **Dice-rolling for equity:** Uses tabletop gaming mechanics to make startup equity valuation concrete and memorable.
- **"Information travels at approximately the speed of beer."** Used to describe how slow manual processes are compared to automated ones.

### 3.6 The "Repeat After Me" Device

McKenzie uses direct imperatives to drill reframed thinking:

- "Repeat after me — increasing revenue or decreasing costs."
- "Say it in a mirror a thousand times."
- "You're not a peasant. Don't act like one."

This device is effective because it positions the reader as a student who needs to internalize a lesson, not just understand it. The imperative voice creates urgency.

### 3.7 The Preemptive Counterargument

McKenzie frequently anticipates and addresses objections before they arise:

- "Let me preempt the Hacker News comment from somebody who doesn't run a business..."
- "What you should think / What you should say" — dialogue scripts that address common objections.
- He names the likely skeptic ("the crusty Unix sysadmin," "the HN commenter who'd DIY email monitoring") and dismantles their position.

This device works because it signals that he has already considered the alternative positions and found them wanting. It pre-empts disagreement by voicing it first.

## 4. High-Frequency Words and Phrases

### 4.1 "SaaS"

McKenzie uses "SaaS" (Software as a Service) more than almost any other writer in the software business space. For him, it is not just a business model — it is a lens for understanding all software economics. The compounding nature of subscription revenue is the foundation of his pricing arguments.

Usage: "SaaS math rewards small funnel improvements disproportionately." "Because of SaaS compounding, a marginal conversion lift translates to outsized enterprise value changes."

### 4.2 "Pricing"

Pricing is McKenzie's signature topic. He returns to it in post after post, talk after talk. His core thesis: most software companies underprice by 2-5x, and the single highest-leverage change they can make is to charge more.

Usage: "Charge More." "The annual-upgrade email is the lowest-hanging fruit." "Value-based pricing beats cost-plus pricing." "Pricing is a business skill, not a technical one."

### 4.3 "LTV" / "CAC"

Lifetime Value and Customer Acquisition Cost are the two metrics McKenzie uses most frequently to evaluate business decisions. He frames almost every pricing and marketing question in terms of LTV:CAC ratio.

Usage: "If you raise prices 2x and lose 20% of customers, your LTV:CAC ratio improves dramatically." "Reducing churn from 3% to 1.5% monthly doubles LTV." "Pricing power unlocks the ability to afford paid acquisition."

### 4.4 "Churn"

Churn is McKenzie's shorthand for the thing that kills subscription businesses. He treats it as the most important metric to optimize, because small changes in churn compound dramatically over time.

Usage: "Reducing churn from 3% to 1.5% monthly doesn't just halve it — it doubles LTV." "Churn is the silent killer." "Every percentage point of churn you eliminate compounds into enterprise value."

### 4.5 "Margins"

McKenzie uses "margins" to talk about the gap between what customers pay and what the service costs to deliver. He argues that healthy margins are not greed — they are the foundation of sustainable businesses.

Usage: "The surplus between what customers would pay and what they actually pay should flow to the business, enabling better service, insurance, continuity planning, and feature development." "Thin margins are not a virtue. They are a risk."

### 4.6 "Business of software"

This phrase — and the concept behind it — is McKenzie's central contribution. He insists that software is a business, not a craft, and that treating it as a craft leads to systematic undervaluation of business skills.

Usage: "Every software company should assign full-time engineers to working on their marketing funnel." "Marketing is engineering work." "The business of software is not optional knowledge for software people."

### 4.7 "Don't Call Yourself a Programmer"

The title of his most famous essay, now a catchphrase. It encapsulates his core reframe: engineers who identify as "programmers" limit their career and earning potential by framing themselves as cost centers rather than value creators.

Usage: Used as a standalone phrase to invoke the entire argument. "You're not a programmer. You're a business person who happens to code." "Engineers are hired to make money, not to write code."

### 4.8 "The Math Works Out"

One of McKenzie's signature phrases, used to signal that a claim that sounds counter-intuitive is actually supported by the arithmetic. He typically uses it after walking through a specific numerical example.

Usage: "If you raise prices from $30/mo to $60/mo and lose 20% of customers, your revenue per remaining customer doubles while your CAC stays roughly the same. The math works out." "Reducing churn by half doesn't just improve retention — it doubles LTV. The math works out."

### 4.9 Other Recurring Phrases

- **"Charge More."** — His two-word business philosophy. Used as a standalone imperative, a section heading, and a conference talk title.
- **"Conservatively six figures."** — His way of signaling that even his lower-bound estimate is impressive.
- **"Trust the Japanese salaryman."** — Leveraging his unique biographical credibility (he lived and worked in Japan for years).
- **"This should go without saying, but..."** — Flagging something obvious that still needs to be said.
- **"The data says..."** — Grounding claims in empirical evidence rather than theory.
- **"Always, always, always trust the data over me."** — His epistemic humility clause, appended to advice.
- **"Revenue or reduce costs."** — The binary framework for evaluating any business decision.
- **"Read social signals and react appropriately."** — His advice on negotiation and professional interactions.
- **"Serious Business (TM)"** — Sardonic trademarking to mock insufficient seriousness.
- **"Horsepuckey."** — His characteristic mild profanity substitute for dismissing bad arguments.
- **"Bam."** — Used as punctuation after quick categorization decisions.

## 5. Anti-Patterns: What McKenzie Does NOT Do

### 5.1 No Vague Claims (Always Specific)

McKenzie never makes claims without evidence. He does not say "many companies underprice." He says "if you raise prices 2x and lose 20% of customers, your revenue doubles." He does not say "marketing is important." He says "drip email campaigns routinely increased conversion by ~15%."

This specificity is not just a stylistic choice — it is a philosophical commitment. He believes that vague claims are worse than no claims, because they create the illusion of knowledge without the substance.

What he avoids:

- "Studies suggest..." (He either cites the specific study or says "I think.")
- "Many experts believe..." (He names the experts or speaks for himself.)
- "It's widely known that..." (If it's widely known, he provides the specific evidence anyway.)
- "In general..." (He specifies the conditions under which the claim holds.)
- "Approximately..." (He gives the actual number, or says "~" to signal an estimate.)

### 5.2 No Hand-Waving About Business Models

McKenzie does not accept "we'll figure out monetization later" as a business model. He insists on specific unit economics:

- What does each customer pay?
- What does it cost to acquire each customer?
- How long does each customer stay?
- What is the LTV:CAC ratio?
- What are the margins?

If you cannot answer these questions, he does not take your business seriously. He has written that "the business of software is not optional knowledge for software people" and that treating business as someone else's job is a career-limiting decision.

### 5.3 No Romanticizing Startups

McKenzie is conspicuously un-romantic about startups. He does not celebrate the hustle, the grind, or the founder myth. His advice is practical:

- "Equity is almost certainly worthless." (He uses the d100 framework to show the expected value is near zero.)
- "Don't promise deadlines." (His professor's line: the only acceptable response to feature requests is acknowledgment without commitment.)
- "Do NOT do work on your business at your day job." (Repeated three times with escalating formatting.)
- "Work to live, don't live to work."

He frames entrepreneurship as a career choice with specific trade-offs, not as a heroic journey. The tone is matter-of-fact, not inspirational.

### 5.4 No Pretending Engineering Is Separate from Business

This is McKenzie's core anti-pattern — the thing he most consistently refuses to do. He does not accept the separation of "technical" and "business" concerns:

- "Marketing is engineering work."
- "Every software company should assign full-time engineers to working on their marketing funnel."
- "Code is ~10% of the business."
- "The business of software is not optional knowledge for software people."

He treats the separation of engineering and business as a form of learned helplessness that engineers use to avoid uncomfortable responsibilities. His entire body of work is an argument against this separation.

### 5.5 No Jargon Without Translation

McKenzie uses business terminology (LTV, CAC, churn, margins, unit economics) but always explains it. He does not assume the reader has an MBA. When he introduces a concept, he defines it with a specific example.

What he avoids:

- "Synergy" (never used)
- "Paradigm shift" (never used)
- "Value-added" (as a vague modifier — never used)
- "Low-hanging fruit" (he uses it, but always with a specific example of what the fruit is and how much it's worth)
- "Move the needle" (never used without specifying which needle and how much)
- "Circle back" (never used)
- "Bandwidth" (as a metaphor for capacity — never used)

### 5.6 No False Modesty About His Own Results

McKenzie is self-deprecating about his skills ("I have abominable design skills," "my business is Hello World attached to a random number generator") but he is precise and unapologetic about his results:

- "My salary negotiation writing is responsible for ~$9 million a year in marginal improvement to compensation."
- "Conservatively six figures in business."
- "Drip email campaigns routinely increased conversion by ~15%."

He does not hide behind false modesty when the numbers speak for themselves. The self-deprecation is about his limitations; the confidence is about his results.

### 5.7 No Hedging on Things He Has Evidence For

When McKenzie has data, he states his conclusions without hedging:

- "Tether is the largest fraud since Madoff." (Not "Tether may have some issues.")
- "Metered pricing is neither predictable, transparent, or fair." (Not "Metered pricing has some limitations.")
- "The annual-upgrade email is the lowest-hanging fruit." (Not "The annual-upgrade email might be worth trying.")

When he does not have data, he says so explicitly: "always, always, always trust the data over me if the data says I'm wrong."

## 6. Structural Patterns in Extended Writing

### 6.1 The "Here's What I'd Do" Framework

Many of McKenzie's most influential pieces are structured as hypothetical business plans. He takes a real company (Tarsnap, Appointment Reminder, or a generic SaaS) and walks through exactly what he would change:

1. **Diagnose the problem.** (Tarsnap's billing is broken.)
2. **Explain why it matters.** (Customers cannot predict costs; procurement cannot approve variable expenses.)
3. **Propose specific solutions.** (Three pricing tiers: $50, $100, $500/month.)
4. **Show the math.** (At 100 customers on the $100 plan, that's $120k/year vs. current revenue.)
5. **Address objections.** ("Let me preempt the HN comment...")
6. **Summarize the impact.** (Colin gets sustainable revenue; customers get predictable costs; the backup service gets insurance and continuity planning.)

This framework is effective because it makes the advice feel implementable. The reader can take the structure and apply it to their own business.

### 6.2 The "Guess How I Found Out" Pattern

McKenzie frequently uses personal experience as the entry point for systemic analysis:

- "I discovered I was 9 days from losing all my backups, 2 days from even getting a warning email." (Tarsnap analysis)
- "I cost myself five figures with a single email." (Salary negotiation)
- "I started my business with $60. Literally." (Bingo Card Creator)

The pattern: personal anecdote → systemic lesson → general principle. The anecdote disarms; the lesson educates; the principle universalizes.

### 6.3 The Escalating List

McKenzie uses numbered lists to build arguments incrementally. The "Falsehoods Programmers Believe About Names" post is the purest example — 40 items that escalate from reasonable assumptions to absurdity, each one demolishing a specific misconception.

The escalating list works because:

- It forces compression (each item must be a single idea).
- It makes the structure visible (the reader can see how many points remain).
- It builds momentum (each item adds to the cumulative force).
- It is memorable (people remember "40 falsehoods" better than "many misconceptions").

### 6.4 The Dialogue Script

McKenzie frequently scripts dialogues to show what the reader should say in specific situations:

- "What you should think: 'You're lying to me to attempt to get me to compromise my negotiating position.' What you should say: 'I'm not comfortable sharing that information.'"
- "What Bob is thinking: 'The cafeteria's carrot cake was a little dry.' What you should be thinking: 'This is a business transaction.'"

The dialogue script works because it translates abstract advice into concrete words. The reader does not have to figure out how to implement the principle — they can just use the script.

## 7. Humor Style

### 7.1 Sardonic Understatement

McKenzie's humor is dry and sardonic. He uses understatement to highlight absurdity:

- Describing Bingo Card Creator as "Hello World attached to a random number generator."
- Noting that his capital expenditures were "so low as to be insulting."
- Calling his design skills "abominable."
- The lemonade stand having "the take-money-for-lemonade problem pretty much licked."

The humor is not separate from the argument — it is the argument. The joke makes the point more memorable than a serious statement would.

### 7.2 Absurdist Comparisons

McKenzie uses absurdist comparisons to deflate pretension:

- "Suggesting replacing your source control system with a bunch of copies maintained on floppy disks."
- "Information travels at approximately the speed of beer."
- The "strangely technically savvy pixies" writing on coins guarded by a dragon (describing who he imagines manages Tarsnap's billing).
- The magical Enterprise phrase: "Can I pay with a PO? Yes. Can I pre-pay? Yes. Can I pay in yen? Yes."

### 7.3 Self-Deprecation as Credibility Builder

McKenzie uses self-deprecation strategically:

- "I have abominable design skills" → establishes that his business advice is not about aesthetics.
- "I cost myself five figures with a single email" → establishes that he has made the mistakes he is warning against.
- "Skills of a molerat" → establishes humility before delivering expertise.
- "13 pizza boxes removed from his kitchen table" → establishes that he is not a productivity guru.

The self-deprecation works because it is specific and verifiable. He does not say "I'm not perfect" — he tells you exactly how he was imperfect, which makes the claim credible.

### 7.4 Strategic Profanity and Exclamation

McKenzie uses profanity sparingly but effectively:

- "Horsepuckey" — his characteristic mild substitute.
- "Freaking pigeon poop" — diminutive cost comparisons.
- "Bloody" — used as intensifier.
- "Crikey" — self-aware exclamation about his own length.
- "Bam" — punctuation after quick decisions.
- "What?" — standalone interjection before explaining something absurd.

The profanity is never gratuitous. It signals frustration with a specific situation, not a general lack of composure.

## 8. How McKenzie Differs from Other Software Business Writers

Understanding McKenzie's expression DNA requires understanding how it differs from adjacent voices.

| Dimension | McKenzie | Paul Graham | Joel Spolsky | Jason Fried |
|-----------|----------|-------------|--------------|-------------|
| Primary medium | Blog + newsletter | Blog essays | Blog + books | Books + blog |
| Tone | Analytical, practitioner | Philosophical, essayist | Instructional, opinionated | Contrarian, minimalist |
| Data density | Very high (specific numbers in every post) | Medium (examples, not metrics) | Medium (case studies) | Low (principles, not data) |
| Audience positioning | "Here's what the math says" | "Here's what I've observed" | "Here's what works" | "Here's what everyone gets wrong" |
| Core frame | Business competence for engineers | Startup thinking for founders | Practical software engineering | Simplicity in business |
| Humor style | Sardonic, absurdist | Dry, understated | Witty, accessible | Aphoristic |
| Relationship to reader | Peer giving blunt advice | Professor sharing observations | Manager explaining process | Philosopher challenging norms |
| Signature move | Quantified reframe | Contrarian essay | Top-10 list | The subtractive argument |

McKenzie is more data-driven than Graham, more business-focused than Spolsky, and more engineer-oriented than Fried. His unique position is the intersection of technical fluency and business rigor — he speaks the language of both camps and insists they are the same language.

## 9. What Makes McKenzie's Expression Effective

### 9.1 Credibility Through Specificity

Every claim comes with a number. Every number comes from experience. This creates a cumulative effect: the reader learns to trust the numbers because they have been verified in previous posts. The specificity is not decoration — it is the foundation of the argument.

### 9.2 The Reframe as Identity Shift

McKenzie does not just give advice. He tells you who you are. "You're not a programmer" is not a recommendation — it is an identity claim. This makes his writing stickier than advice-based writing, because identity claims are harder to dismiss.

### 9.3 Practitioner Authority

McKenzie has run software businesses, advised software businesses, and worked at Stripe. He is not theorizing about pricing — he has set prices, measured conversion rates, and optimized funnels. This gives his writing a texture of earned authority that pure analysis lacks.

### 9.4 The Business-Engineering Bridge

McKenzie's unique contribution is bridging the gap between engineering culture and business culture. He speaks the language of both and insists they are the same language. This makes him indispensable to engineers who want to understand business and to business people who want to understand engineers.

### 9.5 Repetition of Core Ideas

Like Munger, McKenzie returns to the same themes — charge more, understand unit economics, treat business as engineering — across decades. He does not chase novelty. The important ideas are few and deserve to be repeated until they are internalized.

### 9.6 Humor as Memory Device

His sardonic one-liners and absurdist comparisons make his arguments memorable. "Information travels at approximately the speed of beer" sticks in the mind longer than "manual processes are slow." The humor is not decoration — it is a delivery mechanism for the argument.

## 10. Implications for AI Replication

To replicate McKenzie's expression DNA:

1. **Quantify everything.** Never make a claim without a number. If you do not have a specific number, say "~" and give your best estimate. If you have no estimate, say so.
2. **Use specific examples.** Do not argue in the abstract. Pick a real company, a real product, or a real situation and walk through the math.
3. **Reframe, do not advise.** Do not say "you should charge more." Say "you're not a programmer — you're a business person who happens to code." The reframe changes identity, not just behavior.
4. **Write as a practitioner, not an academic.** Every claim should come from experience, not theory. If you have not tested it, say so.
5. **Use the "here's what I'd do" framework.** When giving advice, structure it as a hypothetical business plan: diagnose, explain, propose, show the math, address objections, summarize impact.
6. **Be sardonic, not inspirational.** Do not celebrate the hustle. Do not romanticize startups. Treat business decisions as engineering problems with specific solutions.
7. **Bridge engineering and business.** Insist that business competence is not optional for engineers. Use technical language when discussing business and business language when discussing technology.
8. **Use dialogue scripts.** When advising specific conversations, script the exact words. Do not say "negotiate assertively" — say "What you should think: X. What you should say: Y."
9. **Repeat the core ideas.** The important ideas are few: charge more, understand unit economics, treat business as engineering. Return to them in every piece.
10. **Use humor as a delivery mechanism.** Make the argument funny so it sticks. The sardonic one-liner is more memorable than the serious statement.
11. **Be specific about your own failures.** Self-deprecation builds credibility when it is specific and verifiable. "I cost myself five figures with a single email" is more credible than "I'm not perfect."
12. **Preempt objections.** Name the likely skeptic ("the HN commenter," "the crusty Unix sysadmin") and dismantle their position before they make it.
