# Patrick McKenzie (patio11) -- Conversations, Interviews, and Public Speaking

## Overview

Patrick McKenzie (@patio11) is one of the most prolific and influential voices in the bootstrapped software business community. His public output spans 575+ blog posts on Kalzumeus.com (2006-2025), 14 podcast episodes, multiple conference talks at MicroConf and Business of Software, thousands of Hacker News comments, and extensive Twitter/X threads. His communication style is distinctive: data-dense, blunt, long-form, and deliberately repetitive on core themes.

## Part 2: Conference Talks

### MicroConf 2011: "Software Businesses In 5 Hours A Week"

This talk became one of the most referenced presentations in the bootstrapped software community. Patrick detailed how he ran Bingo Card Creator on approximately 5 hours per week while working full-time as a salaryman in Nagoya, Japan.

**Key framework from the talk**:

1. **Time assets vs. time debt**: Build things that save future time (automation, documentation, scalable marketing) rather than things that create future obligations (buggy software, high-touch sales commitments).

2. **Code is ~10% of the business**: The majority of effort goes to pre-sales inquiries, marketing, SEO, copywriting, and customer support. His product was "Hello World attached to a random number generator" but customers don't care about complexity.

3. **"Complete" is a marketing decision, not an engineering milestone**: Cut features by 50% to ship means launching with 100% of what's implemented.

4. **Wall clock time vs. calendar time**: A/B testing requires 15 minutes of coding but a week of waiting for data -- ideal for part-time operators.

5. **Processes over events**: Instead of handling each support email individually, categorize them and fix root causes through better copy, FAQs, or automation.

### Business of Software 2010

Patrick attended and wrote about his learnings. The conference reinforced his thinking about software pricing and the business model of software companies. His key takeaway: the software industry systematically undervalues what it produces.

### BaconBiz 2013: "Conversion Optimization in Practice"

Detailed presentation on A/B testing and conversion rate optimization for small software companies. Patrick shared specific numbers from Bingo Card Creator and Appointment Reminder.

### TwilioCon 2012: "Selling Your Software To Businesses"

Patrick's presentation on B2B sales for developers who would rather be building. Key argument: enterprise sales is a learnable skill, not an innate talent, and developers are uniquely positioned to understand the technical problems they're solving for customers.

## Part 3: The Blog -- Key Essays and Communication Patterns

### "Don't Call Yourself a Programmer, And Other Career Advice" (October 28, 2011)

This is Patrick's most famous and widely-shared essay. It has been read by millions and fundamentally shaped how many engineers think about their careers.

**Structure**: 17 numbered sections, each addressing a specific misconception. Patrick builds the argument in layers:

1. Establishes the landscape (most software is boring business software)
2. Reframes professional identity (you're a value creator, not a coder)
3. Provides tactical career advice
4. Addresses compensation
5. Discusses interpersonal dynamics
6. Ends with perspective (career isn't everything)

**Key arguments with specific numbers**:

- An internal travel expense form at a 2,000-employee company could save 5,000 man-hours annually at $50/hour fully loaded cost, yielding $250,000 in yearly savings. The technical simplicity of a CRUD app is irrelevant; what matters is the business impact.

- A software engineer earning $100K might generate $1M+ in value for a company. Negotiate accordingly.

- The Google engineer whose bio was writing backend billing code through which 97% of Google's revenue passes -- he later became an angel investor.

- His research advisor offered a spot in his lab, tuition waiver, and $12,000 stipend for 4-6 years of commitment. Market wages for research-capable engineers are $80-100k+, which "buys an awful lot of ramen."

**Memorable quotes**:

> "Your job is not to write code. Your job is to solve business problems. Code is just a means to that end."

> "They hire better mortals, but they still hire mortals."

> "The dominant quality that gets you hired is the ability to give people the perception that you will create value, which isn't necessarily the same as actually creating value."

> "Feeling bad about self-promotion in business is like a baseball batter feeling bad about stealing bases -- you're not morally superior, just playing poorly."

**The Profit Center vs. Cost Center framework**: Patrick invokes Peter Drucker's distinction. Profit Centers bring in revenue. Cost Centers are everyone else, including engineers. Engineers are typically "very highly paid Cost Centers," which incentivizes MBA-driven cost-cutting like outsourcing. His reassurance: nobody outsources Profit Centers.

**On equity**: Uses a d100 (percentile dice) framework:
- 0-70: Worth nothing
- 71-94: Lump sum roughly equivalent to the salary you gave up
- 95-99: Life-changing money
- 100: You worked at the next Google. But 100 doesn't actually appear on a d100.

### "Salary Negotiation: Make More Money, Be More Valued" (January 23, 2012)

Patrick claims this essay has been responsible for "$15M+" in raises attributed to it by readers.

**Opening metaphor**: Would you recite poetry while standing on one foot for $100,000? Of course. He compares this directly to salary negotiation, arguing engineers have turned being bad at negotiation into a "perverse badge of virtue."

**Key numbers**:
- A $5,000 salary bump compounds with annual raises, 401k contributions, and future salary baselines
- Over ten years, $5,000/year extra equals roughly $100,000 gross
- $15,000/year extra (achievable for young engineers) exceeds $100,000 net of taxes over a decade
- A California engineer's fully-loaded cost approaches $20,000/month
- $5,000 feels enormous to you but registers as roughly 3% of fully-loaded cost to HR -- essentially rounding error

**Core tactical advice**:
1. Never give a number first
2. Only negotiate after agreement-in-principle
3. Negotiate across multiple dimensions (vacation, work hours, project assignments)
4. Use the employer's own language back to them
5. Research the company deeply before entering any discussion
6. Read "Getting to Yes" -- negotiation skills are worth thousands per year across an entire career

**On timing**: "Negotiating never makes (worthwhile) offers worse." The worst outcome is getting exactly the original offer.

### "Doing Business In Japan" (November 7, 2014)

One of Patrick's most vivid and detailed essays, covering 20 years of living and working in Japan.

**The salaryman pact**: "You swear yourself to us, body and soul, and in return we will isolate you from all risks." Key features: 90+ hour weeks, 5 vacation days taken per year (from 12-18 allocated), suggested bereavement leave of "two if a parent passes away, and one if your wife passes away."

**On going independent**: When he left his megacorp job, his landlord immediately asked him to leave his apartment. Being "self-employed" ranked "about one half-step above 'homeless vagabond'" in social esteem. His wife Ruriko's friends assumed he was unemployed. The hypothesis was advanced, more than once, that he was "probably a drug dealer."

**The banker named Taro**: Patrick's most vivid business story. A regional bank manager who:
- Correctly intuited when McKenzie started dating and when he broke up based solely on spending patterns
- Prevented a wire transfer during the Lehman shock because he knew, off the top of his head, that the intermediary bank was Wachovia (in FDIC receivership) -- because "it is my business to know this"
- Called the day after the 2011 Tohoku earthquake offering unlimited support: "we'll take care of you and worry about the numbers some other time"
- Left copious handwritten notes about McKenzie's character for his successor

Patrick's balance was below $2,000 for over 8 of his 10 years with the bank. He says no amount of money could make him switch.

**On Japan's market opportunity**: The market is "tremendously underserved" for technology solutions. UX that Silicon Valley would consider "barely adequate for an internal admin app" would strike Japanese customers like "wizardry from the future."

### "Running A Software Business On 5 Hours A Week" (March 20, 2010)

Patrick's framework for part-time entrepreneurship:

**Time assets vs. time debt**: Code that automates a task = time asset. Releasing buggy software = time debt. Even positive activities like high-touch sales can create problematic time debt for part-time operators.

**The iron rule**: Never work on your side business during work hours. Beyond ethics, it creates legal liability.

**On picking problems**: Talk to real people -- carpenters, receptionists, teachers -- about what wastes their time. Look for problems people already spend money solving inefficiently. For bingo cards, he could infer hundreds of thousands of dollars in annual market size just from observing retail shelves.

**On outsourcing**: Stay-at-home parents, retirees, college students, and unemployed professionals in high-wage countries can be excellent, undervalued resources. Pay promptly -- he calls his terms "Net 30 (Minutes)."

### "Stripe And A/B Testing Made Me A Small Fortune" (August 6, 2012)

Patrick documented how switching to Stripe and running A/B tests dramatically increased his revenue. The essay is a masterclass in data-driven decision making.

### "Doubling SaaS Revenue By Changing The Pricing Model" (August 13, 2012)

Specific case study of how changing pricing structure (not just price levels) doubled revenue. Key insight: the $0 to $50 gap is larger than the $50 to $500 gap. Getting someone to pay anything is the hardest step.

### "You Should Probably Send More Email Than You Do" (May 31, 2012)

Patrick's essay on email marketing philosophy. Core argument: most SaaS companies drastically under-communicate with users at critical lifecycle points.

**Key quote**: "People will not give you money if you do not ask them to."

### "Falsehoods Programmers Believe About Names" (June 16, 2010)

A viral essay cataloging incorrect assumptions programmers make about how human names work. It became a template for similar "Falsehoods programmers believe about X" posts across the industry. Demonstrates Patrick's ability to identify blind spots in technical thinking.

### "What Working At Stripe Has Been Like" (October 8, 2020)

Patrick's reflections on joining Stripe to work on Atlas in 2016. He describes the experience of transitioning from solo bootstrapper to employee at a high-growth fintech company.

### "I'm Joining Stripe to Work on Atlas" (September 9, 2016)

Announcement essay explaining why a lifelong bootstrapper would join a company. Patrick framed it as an opportunity to help millions of entrepreneurs incorporate businesses -- scaling his impact beyond what a solo software business could achieve.

## Part 4: Twitter/X Threads and Communication Style

### Recurring Themes in Twitter Threads

Patrick's Twitter presence (@patio11) was characterized by extremely long, detailed threads. Key recurring topics:

1. **Software pricing**: "You are leaving money on the table" was his most repeated refrain. He argued most B2B SaaS companies should multiply prices by 2x-10x.

2. **Career advice for engineers**: Engineers should learn to think in terms of business value, not technical merit.

3. **Enterprise sales**: Six-figure contracts are more accessible than people think. The procurement process at large companies is navigable if you understand the incentive structure.

4. **Financial infrastructure**: After joining Stripe, Patrick became deeply knowledgeable about payments, banking, and financial systems. His "Bits about Money" newsletter continues this focus.

5. **VaccinateCA**: During COVID-19, Patrick helped organize vaccine distribution logistics in California, demonstrating his "engineer as problem-solver" philosophy applied to public health.

### Catchphrases and Recurring Language

- **"You are leaving money on the table"** -- His signature phrase about software pricing
- **"Charge more"** -- The two-word distillation of his pricing philosophy
- **"Boring businesses are the best businesses"** -- Advocating for unglamorous, profitable niches
- **"People will not give you money if you do not ask them to"** -- On email marketing and sales
- **"The thing to understand about X is..."** -- His standard way of introducing key concepts
- **"Conservatively..."** -- Used before providing a lower-bound estimate, signaling he's being deliberately conservative
- **"I have... opinions... on this topic"** -- Understated way of introducing something he's written thousands of words about
- **"This is a solved problem"** -- Dismissing hand-wringing about issues with known solutions

### Argument Structure Pattern

Patrick consistently follows a specific rhetorical pattern:

1. **State the conventional wisdom or engineer's assumption** -- "Most developers think..."
2. **Explain why it's wrong using business logic or real-world examples** -- "However, the actual incentive structure is..."
3. **Provide actionable alternative framing** -- "Instead, you should..."
4. **Support with specific numbers** -- "$X in revenue, Y% conversion rate, Z customers"
5. **Repeat the core message in different contexts** -- He deliberately restates the same lessons across many posts and threads

### Data Usage Pattern

Patrick is unusually specific with numbers. He doesn't say "my revenue increased" -- he says "my revenue increased from $X to $Y over Z months, which represented a W% improvement in conversion rate driven primarily by changing the pricing page layout." His blog posts routinely include:

- Exact revenue figures (down to the dollar)
- Conversion rates at each stage of the funnel
- A/B test sample sizes and confidence intervals
- Customer counts and churn rates
- Time measurements (hours per week, response time in hours)

## Part 6: Storytelling Patterns

### The "Before I Understood X" Narrative

Patrick frequently tells stories about his own naivete before learning a lesson. Example: "I used to describe myself as 'slightly below average' in programming skill. I later realized I had a radically skewed impression of the skill distribution." This pattern makes his advice feel earned rather than preachy.

### The Concrete Example From His Own Business

Nearly every piece of advice is grounded in a specific example from Bingo Card Creator, Appointment Reminder, or his time at Stripe. He doesn't say "you should A/B test" -- he says "I A/B tested my pricing page, changing X to Y, and saw a Z% increase in conversion over N visitors."

### The "I Talked To Someone Who..." Anecdote

Patrick frequently illustrates points with stories about specific people: "One entrepreneur I knew figured out how to sell SaaS door-to-door in Tokyo." "A talented coworker had built Github for SVN as a side project and was hours from launching." These stories ground abstract advice in human reality.

### The Counter-Intuitive Reframe

Patrick's most powerful rhetorical move is taking something engineers assume to be true and flipping it:
- "Your job is not to write code" (counter: engineers think their job IS writing code)
- "Modesty is not career-enhancing" (counter: many engineers believe modesty is virtuous)
- "Co-workers and bosses are not usually your friends" (counter: engineers want to see workplaces as communities)
- "Formal job applications are the exception" (counter: engineers think the resume-submit-interview path is normal)

### The Escalating Specificity Pattern

Patrick starts with a broad claim ("most software companies undercharge"), then narrows to a specific industry ("B2B SaaS"), then to a specific scenario ("a tool used by employees earning $100k+/year"), then to a specific number ("charging $49/month is absurd"). Each layer makes the argument more concrete and actionable.

## Part 7: Evolution of Communication Platform

### Phase 1: Blog (2006-2012) -- The Foundation

The Kalzumeus blog was Patrick's primary platform. Posts were extremely long (2000-5000 words), data-dense, and published 2-4 times per month during peak years. The blog established his core themes: software pricing, conversion optimization, SEO, and running a small software business.

**Key characteristic**: Radical transparency. Patrick published exact revenue numbers, conversion rates, and business metrics. This was unusual in 2006-2010 and built enormous credibility.

### Phase 2: Conference Talks (2010-2014) -- Amplification

MicroConf and Business of Software talks distilled blog-length arguments into 30-60 minute presentations. These talks reached audiences who wouldn't read 5000-word blog posts and established Patrick as a speaker.

### Phase 3: Twitter (2012-2022) -- Brevity and Reach

Twitter forced Patrick to compress his ideas into shorter formats, though he frequently used thread-continuation to maintain his characteristic detail. His Twitter following grew to tens of thousands, amplifying his reach beyond the indie software community.

### Phase 4: Stripe (2016-2022) -- Institutional Authority

Working at Stripe gave Patrick institutional credibility and access to information about financial infrastructure that few independent bloggers had. His writing shifted from "how to run a small software business" to "how the financial system works."

### Phase 5: Newsletter and Podcast (2020-present) -- Current Work

"Bits about Money" (bitsaboutmoney.com) is Patrick's current newsletter about financial infrastructure. "Complex Systems" (complexsystemspodcast.com) is his weekly podcast. These represent a maturation from tactical software advice to systemic analysis of how institutions work.

## Part 8: Key Themes Across All Conversations

### Theme 1: Engineers Undervalue Themselves

This is Patrick's most persistent message. It appears in "Don't Call Yourself a Programmer," his salary negotiation essay, his pricing advice, and countless HN comments. The specific mechanism: engineers think they're paid to write code, when they're actually paid to solve business problems that generate revenue.

### Theme 2: Business Skills Beat Technical Skills

Patrick consistently argues that understanding business context, revenue models, and organizational politics is more important than mastering the latest framework. This is controversial in engineering circles, which is partly why he keeps saying it.

### Theme 3: Specificity Beats Generality

Patrick never says "charge more" without saying "charge $X instead of $Y." He never says "optimize conversion" without saying "change button color from A to B and see a C% increase." This specificity is what makes his advice actionable rather than inspirational.

### Theme 4: The Market Is Bigger Than You Think

Whether discussing software pricing ("your customers' employers have budgets"), career opportunities ("most desirable jobs are filled through personal networks"), or Japan ("130 million globally rich people"), Patrick consistently argues that engineers underestimate the size of available opportunities.

### Theme 5: Systems Beat Willpower

Patrick's "5 hours a week" philosophy is fundamentally about building systems (automation, documentation, processes) rather than relying on effort. His lifecycle email advocacy is about building systems that convert customers automatically rather than hustling for each sale.

### Theme 6: Boring Is Beautiful

Bingo Card Creator for teachers. Appointment Reminder for dentists. Corporate formation paperwork via Stripe Atlas. Patrick consistently gravitates toward unglamorous problems with clear economic value, and he argues this is the optimal strategy for most entrepreneurs.

## Part 9: Communication Style Analysis

### Sentence-Level Patterns

Patrick writes in long, complex sentences with multiple clauses. He favors the em-dash for parenthetical asides. He uses "which" clauses to add specificity without breaking the main thought. Example: "An internal travel expense form at a 2,000-employee company could save 5,000 man-hours annually at $50/hour fully loaded cost, yielding $250,000 in yearly savings."

### Paragraph-Level Patterns

Each paragraph typically follows: claim, evidence, implication. Patrick rarely makes a claim without supporting it, and he rarely presents evidence without drawing a conclusion.

### Essay-Level Patterns

Essays follow a predictable arc: establish a common misconception, dismantle it with evidence, provide an alternative framework, offer actionable steps, and end with perspective. The "Don't Call Yourself a Programmer" essay is the canonical example.

### Tone

- **Blunt but not cruel**: Patrick tells hard truths without being mean
- **Confident but not arrogant**: He writes from experience, not theory
- **Generous but not naive**: He gives away hard-won knowledge while acknowledging the system's harshness
- **Technical but accessible**: He uses business jargon when appropriate but explains it for engineers

### Use of Humor

Patrick uses dry, understated humor. His equity valuation section using d100 dice is a prime example. He also uses self-deprecating humor about his own early naivete, which makes his advice feel earned.
