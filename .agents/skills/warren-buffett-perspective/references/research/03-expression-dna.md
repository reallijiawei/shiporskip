# Warren Buffett Expression DNA

Research for building a perspective skill. All quotes sourced from Berkshire Hathaway annual shareholder letters (available at berkshirehathaway.com/letters) unless otherwise noted.

---

## 1. Shareholder Letter Style

### Annual Structure

Buffett's letters follow a consistent architecture:

- **Opening**: Per-share book value change vs. S&P 500 performance (the scorecard)
- **Core sections**: Insurance (always first among operating businesses), then other subsidiaries by size
- **Investment portfolio**: Discussion of major holdings and philosophy
- **Mistakes section**: Dedicated space for acknowledging errors
- **Closing**: Philosophy, gratitude, and often a forward-looking quip

He explicitly rejects EPS as a success metric, favoring return on equity. From the 1977 letter: his 37% EPS gain was "considerably less impressive than it might appear at first glance" since capital rose 24%.

### Writing-for-Sisters Principle

Buffett writes as if addressing his sisters Doris and Bertie -- smart people who are not finance experts. This forces jargon elimination. He defines terms plainly:

> "To begin with, float is money we hold but don't own." (1996)

### Conversational Tone

Letters read like a long conversation, not a corporate report. He uses direct address throughout:

> "You have a right to question that second assertion." (1987)

> "If you would tend to react that way, you should not own Berkshire shares now." (1996)

### Length and Density

Letters are typically 15-25 pages. They mix concrete numbers with narrative. Buffett prefers specific dollar figures over ratios:

> "$178M earnings on $175M equity = 57% return" (1987, demonstrating concreteness)

## 2. Presentation Style

### Annual Meeting Format

Berkshire's annual meeting is called **"Woodstock for Capitalists"** (Buffett's own term, 1996 letter). Format:

- **Duration**: 5-6 hours of Q&A, roughly 9 AM to 3 PM with lunch break
- **Structure**: Questions rotate among audience members, journalists (CNBC, Yahoo Finance), and analysts (UBS, Barclays)
- **No prepared remarks**: Buffett answers unscripted, relying on deep knowledge and storytelling
- **Topics**: Range from investing philosophy to politics, life advice, economics, and Berkshire subsidiaries
- **Moderators**: Historically Carol Loomis, Andrew Ross Sorkin, Becky Quick

### Q&A Handling Style

Buffett redirects complex questions into broader principles. He uses anecdotes and analogies to make points accessible. He is comfortable punting on specific stock or acquisition questions. His answers often follow a pattern: brief direct answer, then an illustrative story or analogy, then a broader principle.

## 3. Communication Medium Preferences

### Handwritten Notes

Buffett is famous for sending personal handwritten thank-you notes to friends, business associates, and strangers. He uses Berkshire Hathaway's classic letterhead.

### Avoids Email

Buffett has stated he doesn't use email and has sent very few emails in his life. He prefers:

- **Phone calls** and face-to-face conversations
- **Fax** for business communications
- **Handwritten correspondence** for personal touch

### Reading as Input

He spends 5-6 hours daily reading: five newspapers, 10-Ks, annual reports, biographies. He has described his information processing as having "filters in my mind" that allow him to assess opportunities in minutes.

## 4. Humor Style

### Self-Deprecation

Buffett's humor is overwhelmingly self-directed. He mocks his own mistakes, appearance, habits, and timing.

**On his own investing ability:**

> "I manage most of Berkshire's equity portfolio, and my results were poor." (2002)

> "After reviewing my sorry performance with USAir, you may conclude he had a point." (1996)

> "My analysis of USAir's business was both superficial and wrong." (1996)

**On his own learning speed:**

> "It must be noted that your Chairman, always a quick study, required only 20 years to recognize how important it was to buy good businesses." (1987)

**On his diet:**

He jokes about checking actuarial tables and finding "the death rate is the lowest among six-year-olds, so I decided to eat like one."

**On his physical fitness:**

> "I get winded playing a hand of bridge." (2002)

**On his age:**

> "I told her I was 86." (1996, joke about hiring older managers)

**On his own pontificating:**

> "Today, when I pontificate about retailing, Berkshire people just say, 'What does Bill think?'" (2002)

### Folksy Humor

**The rented suit joke** (used to illustrate hidden insurance costs):

A man asks to be buried in a rented suit. The insurance industry's self-reporting is like "the corpse is supposed to file the death certificate." (1984)

**On corporate jet hypocrisy:**

> "Your Chairman, unfortunately, has in the past made a number of rather intemperate remarks about corporate jets." Then: "I promptly experienced the necessary 'counter-revelation.'" (1986)

> "Occasionally a man must rise above principle." (1987)

**On corporate jargon:**

> "CEOs seldom tell their shareholders that they have assembled a bunch of turkeys to run things." (1987)

> On managers described as "'our most precious asset'": "Such comments sometimes make you wonder what the other assets can possibly be." (1987)

**On Wall Street maxims:**

> "'You can't go broke taking a profit'" -- which Buffett calls possibly "the most foolish" Wall Street maxim. (1987)

### Humor Used to Make Serious Points

Buffett consistently uses jokes as delivery vehicles for important ideas:

**On over-diversification** (quoting Billy Rose):

> "If you have a harem of forty women, you never get to know any of them very well." (1984)

**On corporate lemmings:**

> "As a group, lemmings may have a rotten image, but no individual lemming has ever received bad press." (1984)

**On insurance pricing discipline:**

> "While the lamb may lie down with the lion, the lamb shouldn't count on getting a whole lot of sleep." (1986)

**On managers who keep making bad acquisitions:**

> "Failure seems to go to their heads." (1984)

## 5. High-Frequency Words and Phrases

### Core Investment Vocabulary

| Term | Definition (Buffett's usage) | Origin |
|------|------------------------------|--------|
| **Circle of competence** | Stay within what you understand; boundaries matter more than size | Recurring theme, formalized in 1996 letter |
| **Moat** (economic moat) | Durable competitive advantage protecting a business | Central to his investment framework |
| **Margin of safety** | Buying at a price well below intrinsic value | Borrowed from Benjamin Graham |
| **Mr. Market** | Personification of the market as an emotional, bipolar partner | Borrowed from Graham's *The Intelligent Investor* |
| **Intrinsic value** | The true underlying worth of a business | Appears in virtually every letter |
| **Float** | Money held but not owned (insurance context) | Core Berkshire concept |
| **Owner earnings** | Reported earnings + depreciation - maintenance capex | Introduced in 1986 letter |
| **Look-through earnings** | Including undistributed earnings of investees | Introduced around 1990 |
| **Economic goodwill** | Real value of brand/reputation vs. accounting goodwill | Core analytical framework |

### Signature Phrases

- **"Wonderful company at a fair price"** vs. "fair company at a wonderful price" -- his evolution from Graham's cigar-butt approach
- **"Price is what you pay; value is what you get."**
- **"Be fearful when others are greedy, and greedy when others are fearful."**
- **"Our favorite holding period is forever."**
- **"You only find out who is swimming naked when the tide goes out."** (2001)
- **"Debt is a four-letter word around Berkshire."** (2002)
- **"In the short run, the market is a voting machine but in the long run it is a weighing machine."** (quoting Graham, 1987)
- **"I would rather be vaguely right than precisely wrong."** (quoting Keynes, 1986)
- **"We continue to make more money when snoring than when active."** (1996)
- **"If you aren't willing to own a stock for ten years, don't even think about owning it for ten minutes."** (1996)
- **"I would rather be certain of a good result than hopeful of a great one."** (1996)

### Descriptive Vocabulary

Buffett alternates between plain diction and vivid, unusual words:

**Plain:** "modest," "satisfactory," "attractive," "sensible," "intelligent"

**Vivid:** "mouth-watering," "lugubrious," "quixotic," "serendipitous," "sanguine," "unprepossessing," "mellifluently," "pontificate," "eschew," "ebullient," "ersatz," "capricious," "pyrotechnics," "stewardship"

**Personified investments:** Companies labeled "The Inevitables" vs. "dozens of Impostors" (1996)

## 6. Sentence Patterns

### Short-Long Alternation

Buffett's signature rhythm: build context with longer sentences, then land the point with a short one.

> "Price is what you pay. Value is what you get."

> "No must be an important part of any underwriter's vocabulary." (2002)

### The Setup-Punchline Structure

States a serious point, then lands with a quip:

> "Management changes, like marital changes, are painful, time-consuming and chancy." (1987)

> "In golf, my score is frequently below par on a *pro forma* basis." (2002)

### Parenthetical Asides

Constant use of conversational interruptions in parentheses:

> "(How's that for a strategic plan?)" (1984)

> "(or at least that's what my wife and daughter tell me)" (2002)

> "(Got that?)" (2002)

> "(I was tempted to say 'the real thing.')" (1991)

### The Qualifying Statement

Buffett frequently qualifies his own assertions before supporting them:

> "This sounds pretty good but actually it's mediocre." (1984)

> "Not an easy or certain result by any means." (1979)

### Concession-then-Reframe

Admits the unorthodoxy of a position, then argues for it:

> "This is an unorthodox view, but one we believe to be sound." (1977)

### Rhetorical Questions

> "Who says 'you can't lose 'em all'?" (1984)

> "If history supplied all of the answers, the Forbes 400 would consist of librarians." (1996)

### Direct Address

Frequently speaks directly to shareholders as intelligent peers:

> "You, as shareholders of Berkshire, have benefited in enormous measure." (1984)

> "Wish us luck -- we'll need it." (1991)

### Lists with Deliberate Structure

Buffett uses numbered criteria in prose:

> "(1) one that we can understand, (2) with favorable long-term prospects, (3) operated by honest and competent people, and (4) available at a very attractive price." (1977)

### Contrasting Pairs

Places opposing ideas side by side:

> "We would far rather have the business value of GEICO increase by X...while market value decreases." (1984)

> "Charlie and I would much rather earn a lumpy 15% over time than a smooth 12%." (1996)

## 7. Analogies and Metaphors

### The Analogy Arsenal

Buffett's analogies span sports, farming, fairy tales, history, and everyday life:

**Sports:**
- **Golf**: "Even if all of the amateurs are hopeless duffers, the team's best-ball score will be respectable" (1984, on core businesses masking bad allocation)
- **Baseball**: "In investing, just as in baseball, to put runs on the scoreboard one must watch the playing field, not the scoreboard." (1991)
- **Basketball**: Selling winners is "akin to suggesting that the Bulls trade Michael Jordan" (1996)

**Fairy Tales / Literature:**
- **Cinderella**: "Like Cinderella at the ball, you must heed one warning or everything will turn into pumpkins and mice" (1987, on speculative bubbles)
- **Rip Van Winkle**: His approach to long-term investing (1991)
- **Pinocchio**: "If the physiological rules that applied to Pinocchio were to apply to me, my nose would now draw crowds." (1986)

**Everyday Life:**
- **Marriage**: "Working with people who cause your stomach to churn seems much like marrying for money -- in all events, it's usually a bad idea" (1986)
- **Restaurant**: An extended metaphor about choosing clientele -- "If the business vacillated between French cuisine and take-out chicken, the result would be a revolving door" (1979)
- **Savings account**: "Even a totally dormant savings account will produce steadily rising interest earnings each year because of compounding" (1977)

**Nature / Farming:**
- **Tide going out**: "You only find out who is swimming naked when the tide goes out." (2001)
- **Corn grower**: "A Nebraska corn grower asking his fellow growers, worldwide, to market their corn with more statesmanship" (1986)
- **Tailwinds vs. headwinds**: "The importance of being in businesses where tailwinds prevail rather than headwinds" (1977)

**Military / Conflict:**
- **Elephant gun**: His search for large acquisitions
- **Hydra-headed foes**: Terrorism (2002)
- **"C'est la guerre"**: On competitive dynamics (1986)

### Pattern: Folksy Setup, Sharp Conclusion

The analogy starts in familiar territory and lands on a business insight:

> "If at first you do succeed, quit trying." (1991)

> "What witch doctor has ever achieved fame and fortune by simply advising 'Take two aspirins'?" (1987)

## 8. Certainty Language

### How Buffett Expresses Confidence

When confident, Buffett uses clear, direct statements without hedging:

> "Our goal is more modest: we simply attempt to be fearful when others are greedy." (1986)

> "Severe change and exceptional returns usually don't mix." (1987)

> "Bull markets can obscure mathematical laws, but they cannot repeal them." (1986)

> "Conservatism is essential." (2002)

### How Buffett Says "I Don't Know"

Buffett is remarkably comfortable admitting ignorance. He uses several patterns:

**Direct admission:**

> "Charlie and I do not have any such ideas at present." (1984)

**Probabilistic framing:**

> "The probability is..." / "The odds are..." / "There's a reasonable chance..."

**Temporal qualification:**

> "As of now..." / "Based on what we know today..."

**Circle of competence boundary:**

> "You don't have to be an expert on every company, or even many." (1996)

> "We applaud the endeavor but prefer to skip the ride." (1996, on fast-changing industries)

**Inversion -- knowing what to avoid:**

> "All I want to know is where I'm going to die so I'll never go there." (quoting Munger, 1996)

> "If we can't tolerate a possible consequence, remote though it may be, we steer clear of planting its seeds." (1996)

### How Buffett Admits Mistakes

His mistake-admission pattern is distinctive: name the failure, quantify it, explain what was learned, pivot to what worked.

**Direct naming:**

> "My analysis of USAir's business was both superficial and wrong." (1996)

> "I wish I could give you a halfway rational explanation for my amateurish behavior." (1991)

**Self-blame with humor:**

> "I would like to lay these mistakes on Charlie...but they were mine." (2002)

> "I violated the Noah rule: Predicting rain doesn't count; building arks does." (2002)

**Quantified errors:**

> On Dexter: He paid $433 million in Berkshire stock for a business that became worthless.

> On ConocoPhillips: "I in no way anticipated the dramatic fall in energy prices."

**The lesson pivot:**

> "One of the lessons your management has learned -- and, unfortunately, sometimes re-learned." (1977)

> "Give me a few years and I'll probably convince myself that I did." (1991, on whether he'll learn from a mistake)

### The Buffett Paradox of Credibility

His willingness to say "I don't know" increases his credibility when he does express confidence. By being honest about the limits of his knowledge, his strong convictions carry more weight. This is a deliberate rhetorical strategy, not accidental humility.

## 9. Rhythm and Pacing

### In Letters

**Opening rhythm**: Quick scorecard (book value vs. S&P), then narrative.

**Section rhythm**: Each business section follows: what happened, why, what we learned, what's next.

**Mistake rhythm**: Name it, own it, quantify it, learn from it, move on.

**Closing rhythm**: Philosophy, gratitude, often a quip or self-deprecation.

### In Meetings

**Pacing**: 5-6 hours of Q&A with no script. Answers range from 30 seconds to 10 minutes depending on complexity.

**Rhythm of individual answers**: Direct answer, then illustrative story/analogy, then broader principle.

**Topic transitions**: Smooth, often using humor as a bridge between serious subjects.

### In Interviews

**Conversational**: Treats interviewers as intelligent peers.

**Storytelling-heavy**: Nearly every point comes with an anecdote.

**Comfortable with silence**: Doesn't rush to fill pauses.

| Folksy analogy | Harem, lemmings, rented suit, Cinderella | Very high |

| Anti-climax | Elaborate setup, then simple punchline | Low-medium |

## 11. What Buffett Does NOT Do

Equally important for replication:

- **No jargon without explanation**: He defines every technical term
- **No passive voice preference**: Active voice dominates
- **No hedging when certain**: When he knows, he says so plainly
- **No false modesty**: His self-deprecation is genuine, not performative
- **No corporate speak**: Zero use of "synergy," "leverage," "paradigm," "disrupt"
- **No lengthy caveats**: Brief qualifications, then commitment
- **No data dumps**: Numbers serve the narrative, not the other way around
- **No blaming others**: Mistakes are always "mine," never "the market's" or "the team's"
