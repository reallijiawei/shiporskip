## 1. Poor Charlie's Almanack

### Overview

First published in 2005, with a third edition in 2023 (posthumous, Munger died November 28, 2023). Edited by Peter D. Kaufman. The definitive compilation of Munger's speeches, talks, and accumulated wisdom. Not a traditional book Munger wrote himself — it is a curated collection of his public appearances, annotated and organized by Kaufman.

### Structure and Key Content

The book contains 11 speeches and talks spanning from 1986 to 2007, interspersed with commentary on Munger's life, philosophy, and methods. The 2023 edition added new material and reflections.

### Core Ideas from the Almanack

#### The Latticework of Mental Models

Munger's most enduring contribution to thinking about thinking. The idea that you must build a lattice of models from multiple disciplines — not just one field — to properly understand reality.

> "You've got to have models in your head. And you've got to array your experience — both vicarious and direct — on this latticework of mental models."

> "You may have noticed students who just try to remember and pound back what is remembered. Well, they fail in school and in life. You've got to hang experience on a latticework of models in your head."

The key disciplines Munger draws from:
- **Psychology** (especially cognitive biases)
- **Microeconomics** (especially scale advantages)
- **Biology/Evolution** (adaptation, survival)
- **Physics** (critical mass, chain reactions)
- **Mathematics** (probability, combinatorics)
- **Engineering** (redundancy, margin of safety)
- **History** (patterns, precedent)

#### The "Multiple Models" Approach

> "What are the big models? The first rule is that you've got to have multiple models — because if you just have one or two that you're using, the nature of human psychology is such that you'll torture reality so that it fits your models, or at least you'll think it does."

> "The second rule is that you've got to get the models into your head and use them — not just know about them. And the third rule is that you've got to use them all the time — it's not enough to just know them."

#### Elementary Worldly Wisdom

From his 1996 speech at Stanford, Munger outlined what he calls "elementary worldly wisdom":

> "Well, the first rule is that you can't really know anything if you just remember isolated facts and try and bang 'em back. If the facts don't hang together on a latticework of theory, you don't have them in a usable form."

> "You've got to have multiple models. And the models have to come from multiple disciplines — because all the wisdom of the world is not to be found in one little academic department."

#### Avoiding Stupidity Over Seeking Brilliance

> "It is remarkable how much long-term advantage people like us have gotten by trying to be consistently not stupid, instead of trying to be very intelligent."

> "I constantly see people rise in life who are not the smartest, sometimes not even the most diligent, but they are learning machines. They go to bed every night a little wiser than they were when they got up."

> "The big money is not in the buying and selling, but in the waiting."

## 2. The Psychology of Human Misjudgment

### Overview

Originally a speech given by Munger at Harvard University in 1995. Later revised and expanded. This is arguably Munger's most important intellectual contribution — a systematic catalog of the psychological tendencies that cause human misjudgment. He identified 25 cognitive biases/tendencies, organized as a checklist for avoiding errors.

### The 25 Tendencies (With Commentary)

#### 1. Reward and Punishment Superresponse Tendency

The most powerful force in human behavior. People respond to incentives far more powerfully than most realize.

> "Never, ever, think about anything else when you should be thinking about the power of incentives."

> "I think I've been in the top 5% of my age cohort all my life in understanding the power of incentives, and all my life I've underestimated it."

Example: Federal Express night shift — when they switched from paying by the hour to paying by the shift, the sorting problem was solved overnight.

#### 2. Simple, Pain-Avoidance Psychological Denial

> "The reality is too painful to bear, so you just distort it until it's bearable."

#### 3. Avoidance-Tendency

People avoid unpleasant facts and thoughts.

#### 4. Social-Proof Tendency

The tendency to do what others are doing, especially under conditions of uncertainty.

> "Social-proof tendency — this is a very important tendency — it's what causes manias. It's what causes the lemmings to go over the cliff."

#### 5. Contrast-Misreaction Tendency

People judge things by contrast rather than absolute value. A $500 item seems cheap next to a $1,000 item, even if $500 is objectively expensive.

> "A small scale version of this contrast-misreaction tendency is the way you get people to buy expensive things by first showing them even more expensive things."

#### 6. Stress-Influence Tendency

Under extreme stress, people's cognitive abilities degrade dramatically.

#### 7. Availability-Misweighing Tendency

> "The brain cannot weigh what it cannot retrieve. The thing that comes most easily to mind is overweighted."

#### 8. Authority-Misinfluence Tendency

People defer too readily to perceived authorities.

> "Practically everybody, when he sees something attributed to an authority, tends to believe it, particularly if the authority is someone he respects."

#### 9. Lollapalooza Tendency

The tendency for multiple biases to combine and produce extreme outcomes. When several psychological tendencies operate simultaneously, the result can be far greater than the sum of the parts.

> "When two, three, or four of these tendencies are operating in the same direction, you get lollapalooza effects — and these are the big, catastrophic events."

#### 10. Deprival-Superreaction Tendency

Loss aversion — people feel losses roughly twice as intensely as equivalent gains.

> "A person who has had something almost within his grasp and then lost it can go half crazy."

#### 11. Envy/Jealousy Tendency

> "Envy is the only one of the seven deadly sins that is no fun at all."

#### 12. Drug-Influence Tendency

#### 13. Misgiving-Tendency (Senescence-Misinfluence)

#### 14. Authority-Misinfluence Tendency

#### 15. Twaddle Tendency

People produce and consume meaningless talk.

#### 16. Reason-Respecting Tendency

> "Even one reason can make a person do almost anything — provided the reason is presented in a way that seems to make sense."

#### 17. Liking/Loving Tendency

People distort reality to favor those they love.

#### 18. Disliking/Hating Tendency

People distort reality to harm those they hate.

#### 19. Influence-From-Mere-Association Tendency

People make associations that aren't logically valid — e.g., judging a product by its packaging rather than its contents.

#### 20. Simple, Pain-Avoiding Denial

#### 21. Excessive Self-Regard Tendency

> "People tend to overvalue themselves, their possessions, and their ideas."

> "The average person thinks he's above average. That's mathematically impossible, but psychologically it's universal."

#### 22. Overoptimism Tendency

> "What a crazy way to behave — and yet everybody does it."

#### 23. Deprival-Superreaction Tendency

#### 24. Social-Approval Tendency

#### 25. Contrast-Misreaction Tendency

### Key Insight: Checklist Approach

Munger advocated using these tendencies as a checklist — the way a pilot uses a preflight checklist — to systematically guard against misjudgment.

> "You should develop a checklist of the psychological tendencies — and use it whenever you're making an important decision."

> "I use a kind of two-track analysis. First, what are the factors that really govern the interests involved? Second, what are the subconscious influences where the brain at a subconscious level is automatically forming conclusions?"

## 3. Berkshire Hathaway Annual Letters & Meetings

### Munger's Role

Munger was Vice Chairman of Berkshire Hathaway from 1978 until his death in 2023. While Warren Buffett wrote the annual letters, Munger's influence is evident throughout. At annual meetings, Munger was famous for his brief, often devastating comments.

### Key Contributions to Berkshire Philosophy

#### Float as a Weapon

The concept of using insurance float (premiums collected before claims are paid) as a source of zero-cost or negative-cost capital for investments.

#### Quality Over Cheapness

Munger pushed Buffett away from buying mediocre businesses at cheap prices ("cigar butts") toward buying excellent businesses at fair prices.

> "Forget about buying at a discount. Buy the best businesses and hold them forever."

> "It's not about buying dollar bills for 50 cents. It's about buying dollar bills that are going to become two dollars."

This was a transformative influence. Buffett has credited Munger with changing his investment approach:

> "Charlie shoved me in the direction of not just buying bargains, as Ben Graham had taught me. This was the real impact he had on me. It took a powerful force to move me from the Graham doges. That was the power of Charlie's mind." — Warren Buffett

#### Circle of Competence

> "We have three baskets: in, out, and too tough. We have to have a special insight, or we'll put it in the 'too tough' basket."

> "You have to figure out what your own aptitudes are. If you play games where other people have the aptitudes and you don't, you're going to lose."

### Famous Berkshire Meeting Comments

At annual meetings, Munger's role was often the one who said what Buffett wouldn't — or distilled Buffett's lengthy explanations into a single sentence.

Typical Munger interjections:

> "I have nothing to add."

> "Warren has said it all."

> "I think that's exactly right."

> "I think that's a very intelligent question." (when he didn't want to answer)

When he did speak at length, it was often with blunt force:

> "The world is full of foolish gamblers, and they will not do as well as the patient investor."

> "Knowing what you don't know is more useful than being brilliant."

> "People are trying to be smart — all I am trying to do is not to be idiotic, but it's harder than most people think."

## 4. Daily Journal Annual Meetings

### Overview

Munger served as Chairman of the Daily Journal Corporation, a legal publishing company based in Los Angeles. The annual meetings became legendary for Munger's wide-ranging commentary on investing, life, and human nature.

### Key Themes from Daily Journal Meetings

#### On China

Munger was notably bullish on China and Chinese companies, particularly BYD and Alibaba (though he later sold the Alibaba position).

> "I think China is a great civilization. They've figured out how to have a reasonably modern economy without having a fully free-enterprise system. I think it's remarkable what they've done."

> "I'm more comfortable investing in China than most people. The companies I've invested in are strong, and the people running them are capable."

#### On Cryptocurrency

Munger was famously hostile toward cryptocurrency:

> "I wish it had been banned immediately, and I admire the Chinese for banning it."

> "Bitcoin is disgusting and contrary to the interests of civilization."

> "In my life, I try and avoid things that are stupid and evil and make me look bad in comparison to somebody else — and bitcoin does all three."

> "Crypto is like a venereal disease. I'm proud of the fact that I've avoided it."

#### On Technology Companies

> "I was not smart enough to figure out Google. The fact that it was going to be so dominant in search advertising — I should have figured that out."

> "I was too dumb to realize that Amazon would be as dominant as it turned out to be."

> "I would have been better off if I'd bought Apple earlier. But at least we bought it eventually."

#### On Not Being a Victim

> "The best way to get what you want is to deserve what you want. It's such a simple idea. It's the golden rule. You want to deliver to the world what you would buy if you were on the other end."

> "Spend each day trying to be a little wiser than you were when you woke up."

#### On Life Philosophy

> "I believe in the discipline of mastering the best that other people have ever figured out. I don't believe in just sitting down and trying to dream it all up yourself. Nobody's that smart."

> "You don't have to be brilliant, only a little bit wiser than the other guys, on average, for a long, long time."

#### On Getting Old

> "The best armor of old age is a well-spent life preceding it."

> "I'm not entitled to have an opinion on this subject unless I can state the arguments against my position better than the people who support it."

## 5. Wesco Annual Meetings

### Overview

Munger was Chairman of Wesco Financial Corporation from 1984 until it was fully absorbed by Berkshire Hathaway in 2011. The Wesco annual meetings were smaller, more intimate affairs than Berkshire's, and Munger was even more candid.

### Key Themes from Wesco Meetings

#### On Institutional Behavior

> "Institutional behavior is like the behavior of a bunch of lemmings. Each one does what the others do, and it never occurs to any of them that what they're doing might be stupid."

#### On Corporate Governance

> "I think the system of having boards of directors that are just a bunch of pals of the CEO is a terrible system."

#### On Wall Street

> "Wall Street is the only place that people ride to in a Rolls Royce to get advice from people who take the subway."

> "The investment business has become more and more like a gambling business, and that's not good for the country."

#### On Financial Engineering

> "I think there's too much financial engineering. People are spending too much time on financial engineering and not enough time on making better products."

> "Derivatives are like hell — it's easy to get into and almost impossible to get out of."

#### On Accounting

> "I think there's too much gaming of accounting numbers. People are spending too much time on accounting games and not enough time on making real profits."

## 6. Key Speeches

### "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment" (1995, Harvard)

Already covered in detail in Section 2. The foundational speech.

### "A Lesson on Elementary, Worldly Wisdom As It Relates To Investment Management & Business" (1996, Stanford)

This speech laid out Munger's framework for how to think about business and investing using multiple mental models.

Key ideas:

> "I've long believed that a certain system — which almost any idiot can learn — works way better than the systems that most people are using. You need to have a latticework in your head."

> "You need to have models in your head, and you need to array your vicarious and actual experience on this latticework of models."

The speech uses the example of how to think about Coca-Cola's business success through multiple lenses: psychology (Pavlovian association), biology (survival of the fittest), economics (scale advantages), and mathematics (compound growth).

### "Practical Thought About Practical Thought?" (1996)

A brilliant speech about how to think clearly and practically. Munger uses the example of Coca-Cola to demonstrate how to build a business from scratch using mental models.

The five-step process he outlines:

1. **Simple, easy-to-understand idea**: Start with a big, simple idea.
2. **Pricing**: Get the pricing right.
3. **Distribution**: Make it available everywhere.
4. **Creating desire**: Use psychology to create demand.
5. **Competitive advantage**: Build a moat.

> "Simplify the problem. What are the big models? Simplify the problem by using the big models."

### "The Five Wishes for Wesco" (2003)

Munger outlined five wishes for Wesco Financial:
1. No financial ruin
2. Delightful old age
3. Long and happy marriage
4. Good friends
5. Be worthy of respect

### "Academic Economics" (2003, University of California, Santa Barbara)

Munger critiqued academic economics for being too narrow and too mathematical.

> "Economics is too important to be left to the economists."

> "I think there's a lot wrong with academic economics. It's too much influenced by the desire for mathematical elegance and not enough influenced by the messy reality of the real world."

### "The Causes of Human Misjudgment" (Updated Version, 2005)

An expanded version of the 1995 Harvard speech, with additional examples and refined analysis.

## 7. Investment Philosophy

### Core Principles

#### Circle of Competence

> "You have to figure out what your own aptitudes are. If you play games where other people have the aptitudes and you don't, you're going to lose. You have to figure out where you've got an edge, and you've got to play within your circle of competence."

> "If you know the edge of your own competency, you're a long way ahead of most people."

#### Margin of Safety

Borrowed from Benjamin Graham but applied more broadly:

> "The margin of safety is the most important concept in investing. You should always buy things that are worth more than you pay for them."

> "All intelligent investing is value investing — acquiring more than you are paying for. You must value the business in order to value the stock."

#### Avoiding Catastrophic Loss

> "The first rule of compounding: Never interrupt it unnecessarily."

> "The idea of getting a long-term advantage by being consistently not stupid, instead of being very intelligent — that's a very powerful idea."

> "Invert, always invert. Turn a situation or problem upside down. Look at it backward."

#### The Waiting Game

> "The big money is not in the buying or selling, but in the waiting."

> "Sitting on your ass — that's the key to investing. You do nothing, and then when there's nothing to do, you do it with great vigor."

> "It takes character to sit with all that cash and do nothing. I didn't get to where I am by going after mediocre opportunities."

#### On Diversification

Munger was famously against over-diversification:

> "The idea of excessive diversification is madness."

> "Wide diversification, which necessarily includes investment in mediocre businesses, only guarantees ordinary results."

> "The people who get rich are the people who find a few things that work and concentrate on them."

#### On Market Efficiency

> "The market is not perfectly efficient. It's approximately efficient a lot of the time, but there are times when it's wildly inefficient."

> "If markets were always efficient, I'd be a bum on the street with a tin cup."

#### On Index Funds

Despite his belief in beating the market, Munger acknowledged:

> "For most people, the best thing to do is to own a low-cost index fund."

> "A majority of the people in the investment business are not very good at it. And most of them would be better off in index funds."

#### On Finding Opportunities

> "The way to get rich is to keep your eyes open for opportunities that others miss."

> "You're looking for a mispriced gamble. That's what investing is. And you have to know enough to know whether the gamble is mispriced."

### Investment Decision Checklist

Munger's approach to evaluating investments:

1. **Can I understand the business?** (Circle of competence)
2. **Does it have a durable competitive advantage?** (Moat)
3. **Is the management honest and capable?** (People)
4. **Is the price right?** (Margin of safety)
5. **What could go wrong?** (Inversion)
6. **Am I being influenced by any psychological biases?** (Checklist)

## 8. Communication Style & Personality

### Characteristics

#### Terse and Direct

Munger was famous for short, devastating responses. He didn't waste words.

> "I have nothing to add."

> "Warren has said it all."

> "I think that's exactly right."

> "I think that's a very intelligent question." (when he didn't want to answer)

#### Acerbic and Witty

Munger's humor was sharp, often at the expense of those he considered foolish:

> "I try to get rid of people who always confidently answer questions about which they don't have any real knowledge."

> "If you want to get smart, the question you have to keep asking is: why? why? why? why?"

> "I never allow myself to have an opinion on anything that I don't know the other side's argument better than they do."

#### Self-Deprecating

Despite his intellectual confidence, Munger was often self-deprecating:

> "I'm not entitled to have an opinion on this subject unless I can state the arguments against my position better than the people who support it."

> "I'm not very smart. I just know the limits of my intelligence."

> "I've been so far wrong so many times that I'm used to it."

#### Philosophical

Munger often elevated discussions to philosophical principles:

> "The best armor of old age is a well-spent life preceding it."

> "Spend each day trying to be a little wiser than you were when you got up."

> "The best thing a human being can do is to help another human being know more."

### Communication Patterns

#### The "Lollapalooza" Effect

Munger loved using the word "lollapalooza" to describe situations where multiple forces combine to create extreme outcomes. This became his signature term.

#### The Checklist Approach

Munger constantly advocated checklists — for investing, for thinking, for avoiding errors. This checklist mentality pervades his communication.

#### The "Inversion" Technique

Munger frequently used inversion — turning a question around to look at it from the opposite direction:

> "Tell me where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there."

> "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there."

> "Invert, always invert. Turn a situation or problem upside down. Look at it backward."

#### The "Too Hard" Pile

Munger was famous for putting things in the "too hard" pile — admitting when something was beyond his competence or not worth the effort:

> "We have three baskets: in, out, and too tough. We have to have a special insight, or we'll put it in the 'too tough' basket."

> "Part of the art of investing is knowing what to put in the 'too hard' basket."

### Signature Phrases

- "I have nothing to add."
- "Lollapalooza"
- "Invert, always invert"
- "Too tough"
- "I'm not entitled to an opinion"
- "Spend each day trying to be a little wiser"
- "The big money is in the waiting"

## Appendix B: Munger on Specific Topics

### On Education

> "I think the best way to teach is to have the students do a lot of problems and think about them."

> "The most important thing in education is to be able to think for yourself. If you can't think for yourself, you're going to be at the mercy of other people."

> "I've always believed that the best way to learn is to read a lot. I've never met anyone who was wise who didn't read a lot."

### On Corporate Culture

> "The culture of a company is enormously important. And it's set at the top."

> "You want to have a company where people are honest, where they work hard, and where they're smart. That's not too much to ask."

> "I think the most important thing in business is to have a good culture. If you have a good culture, you'll attract good people."

### On Simplicity

> "Take a simple idea and take it seriously."

> "We have a habit of saying that our best ideas are the ones that are the simplest."

> "Simplify the problem. What are the big models? Simplify the problem by using the big models."

### On Reading

> "In my whole life, I have known no wise people who didn't read all the time — none, zero. You'd be amazed at how much Warren reads — and at how much I read."

> "I constantly see people rise in life who are not the smartest, sometimes not even the most diligent, but they are learning machines. They go to bed every night a little wiser than when they got up, and boy does that help — particularly when you have a long run ahead of you."

> "I don't think you can get to be a really good investor over a broad range without doing a massive amount of reading."

### On Ethics

> "The safest way to get what you want is to try to deserve what you want. It's such a simple idea. It's the golden rule."

> "You want to deliver to the world what you would buy if you were on the other end."

### On Partnership

> "The best partnerships are ones where each partner brings something the other doesn't have."

> "Warren and I have had this wonderful partnership for over 60 years. We've never had an argument. That's because we're both rational."

> "If you want to find a good partner, look for someone who's honest, who's smart, and who works hard. That's all you need."

### On Risk

> "The most important thing in investing is not to lose money. The second most important thing is not to forget the first rule."

> "You should always have a margin of safety. If you think something is worth a dollar, pay 50 cents for it."

> "The biggest risk is not knowing what you're doing."

### On Patience

> "The big money is not in the buying and selling, but in the waiting."

> "It takes character to sit with all that cash and do nothing. I didn't get to where I am by going after mediocre opportunities."

> "The most important thing in investing is patience. If you don't have patience, you're going to lose."

### On Mental Models

> "You've got to have models in your head. And you've got to array your experience — both vicarious and direct — on this latticework of mental models."

> "The first rule is that you can't really know anything if you just remember isolated facts and try and bang 'em back. If the facts don't hang together on a latticework of theory, you don't have them in a usable form."

> "You need to have multiple models — because if you just have one or two that you're using, the nature of human psychology is such that you'll torture reality so that it fits your models, or at least you'll think it does."

### On Competition

> "The best way to compete is to avoid competition. Find a niche where you can be the best."

> "If you're competing against someone who's better than you, you're going to lose. Find something where you have an edge."

> "The best businesses are the ones that have a moat around them. If you can find a business with a moat, you've found a good investment."

### On Innovation

> "I think the best way to innovate is to take existing ideas and combine them in new ways."

> "The most important innovations are often the simplest ones."

> "If you want to be innovative, you have to be willing to be wrong. Most people aren't willing to be wrong."

## Appendix F: Munger's Thinking Framework in Practice

### Example 1: Evaluating a Business

1. **Circle of Competence**: Do I understand this business?
2. **Incentives**: Are the managers incentivized correctly?
3. **Competitive Advantage**: Does this business have a moat?
4. **Margin of Safety**: Is the price right?
5. **Inversion**: What could go wrong?
6. **Checklist**: Am I being influenced by any psychological biases?

### Example 2: Making a Decision

1. **Define the problem clearly**
2. **Consider all the relevant factors**
3. **Apply mental models from multiple disciplines**
4. **Check for psychological biases**
5. **Consider the opposite viewpoint**
6. **Make a decision and act on it**

### Example 3: Avoiding Errors

1. **Identify the most common errors in the situation**
2. **Create a checklist to avoid those errors**
3. **Use inversion to think about what could go wrong**
4. **Seek disconfirming evidence**
5. **Be willing to change your mind**
