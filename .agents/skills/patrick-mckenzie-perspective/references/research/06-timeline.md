# Patrick McKenzie (@patio11) — Comprehensive Life & Career Timeline

## Overview

Patrick McKenzie is an American software engineer, writer, entrepreneur, and fintech commentator who has spent over two decades building software businesses, writing influentially about the economics of software, and working on financial infrastructure. Known online as "patio11," he is considered one of the most influential voices on software pricing, developer career strategy, and the mechanics of small internet businesses.

**Key stats (self-reported, 2025):**
- 575+ essays published since 2006
- ~4.7 million words written
- 20 years living and working in Japan
- Currently based in Chicago

- **Context:** McKenzie grew up in the American Midwest, developing an early interest in computers and programming.
- **Impact:** His Midwest roots and later experience as an outsider in Japan would shape his pragmatic, observation-driven approach to business and culture.

- **Date:** ~2004
- **Event:** Joined the JET (Japan Exchange and Teaching) Programme as an Assistant Language Teacher (ALT)
- **Context:** The JET Programme is a Japanese government initiative that brings native English speakers to Japan to teach English in public schools. McKenzie moved to Japan, initially working as an English teacher.
- **Impact:** This move to Japan would define the next two decades of his life. Being an outsider in a highly structured, consensus-driven culture gave him a unique perspective on business, communication, and systems thinking. The experience of navigating a foreign bureaucracy and business culture deeply influenced his writing style and analytical framework.

## 2006: Kalzumeus Software Founded, Blog Begins

### Early 2006: Starting the Blog

- **Date:** 2006
- **Event:** Founded Kalzumeus Software, LLC and started the Kalzumeus blog (kalzumeus.com)
- **Context:** While still living in Japan and working in the Japanese tech industry, McKenzie began writing about software business, pricing, and the economics of running small internet companies. The name "Kalzumeus" derives from the Japanese word for "squid" (ika) — a reference to his life in Japan.
- **Impact:** This blog would become one of the most influential in the software entrepreneurship space, eventually spanning 575+ essays and ~4.7 million words. His early posts established the core themes: software pricing, marketing for developers, and the gap between how engineers think about value and how the market actually works.

---

### 2006: Working in the Japanese Tech Industry

- **Date:** 2006
- **Event:** Working as a software developer at a small Japanese company
- **Context:** McKenzie was employed in the Japanese corporate world, experiencing firsthand the differences between Japanese and Western business culture — hierarchy, consensus decision-making, lifetime employment, and the role of software engineers in Japanese companies.
- **Impact:** His observations about Japanese business practices (documented in posts like "Doing Business in Japan") became some of his most popular early writing. The contrast between Japanese and American approaches to work, career, and value creation sharpened his thinking about what software engineers should actually optimize for.

## 2007-2009: Early Blogging and Bingo Card Creator

### ~2007: First Software Product

- **Date:** ~2007
- **Event:** Started building and selling software products on the side
- **Context:** While continuing to work in Japan, McKenzie began experimenting with small software products — a common path for developers seeking to build independent income streams.
- **Impact:** This experimentation laid the groundwork for his most famous product and established the pattern of building, marketing, and iterating on small software businesses that would define his career.

---

### ~2007-2008: Bingo Card Creator

- **Date:** ~2007-2008
- **Event:** Created and launched Bingo Card Creator
- **Context:** Bingo Card Creator was a simple software tool that generates custom bingo cards for classroom use, primarily sold to elementary school teachers. McKenzie noticed the need while working as an English teacher in Japan. The product was sold as downloadable software, initially for a modest price.
- **Impact:** Bingo Card Creator became one of the most famous case studies in the indie software / micro-SaaS world. McKenzie wrote extensively about building and marketing it on his blog, covering topics like:
  - SEO for niche products
  - Google AdWords marketing
  - Pricing experiments and A/B testing
  - Customer acquisition for small software businesses
  - The economics of selling to a niche market
  - Conversion optimization
  - The "long tail" of software businesses
- The product demonstrated that a developer could build a viable business selling a simple tool to a small, well-defined market — a lesson that would influence thousands of indie hackers.

---

### 2008-2009: Deepening the Blog

- **Date:** 2008-2009
- **Event:** Regular blog writing on software business topics
- **Context:** McKenzie's blog became increasingly detailed and practical, covering topics like:
  - How to price software products
  - Email marketing for small businesses
  - SEO and paid acquisition strategies
  - Running a software business from Japan
  - The economics of SaaS vs. one-time purchases
- **Impact:** These posts established McKenzie as a go-to resource for developers who wanted to understand the business side of software. His writing was distinctive because it combined deep technical understanding with business acumen, backed by real data from his own experiments.

## 2010: The "Falsehoods" Post and Growing Influence

### June 17, 2010: "Falsehoods Programmers Believe About Names"

- **Date:** June 17, 2010
- **Event:** Published "Falsehoods Programmers Believe About Names"
- **Context:** This post listed dozens of assumptions that programmers routinely encode into systems about human names — assumptions that are almost all wrong (e.g., "names are unique," "names always fit in a certain number of characters," "people have exactly one name," "names don't change," etc.).
- **Impact:** This became one of the most influential blog posts in software engineering history. It spawned an entire genre of "Falsehoods programmers believe about..." posts covering topics like addresses, time, gender, email, phone numbers, and more. The post demonstrated McKenzie's ability to identify systemic blind spots in how software developers think — a skill that would define his later work on financial infrastructure and regulatory systems.

---

### 2010: Growing Reputation

- **Date:** 2010
- **Event:** McKenzie's blog and Hacker News presence grew significantly
- **Context:** His detailed, data-driven posts about running Bingo Card Creator, combined with his distinctive voice on Hacker News (where "patio11" became a recognizable handle), established him as one of the most respected voices in the software entrepreneurship community.
- **Impact:** His Hacker News comments became nearly as influential as his blog posts. He was known for long, detailed comments that combined business insight with technical depth — often explaining how systems actually work beneath the surface.

## 2011-2012: The Career Advice Era

### ~2011: Appointment Reminder Launched

- **Date:** ~2011
- **Event:** Launched Appointment Reminder, a SaaS product
- **Context:** Appointment Reminder was an automated service that sent appointment reminders (via phone, SMS, and email) to reduce no-shows for professional service businesses like medical offices, dental practices, hair salons, and other appointment-based businesses. McKenzie built this as a more scalable SaaS business after the success of Bingo Card Creator.
- **Impact:** Appointment Reminder became another key case study in his writing. The product addressed a real, measurable pain point (no-shows cost businesses significant revenue) and demonstrated the viability of "boring" SaaS businesses that solve mundane but important problems.

---

### October 28, 2011: "Don't Call Yourself A Programmer, And Other Career Advice"

- **Date:** October 28, 2011
- **Event:** Published "Don't Call Yourself A Programmer, And Other Career Advice"
- **Context:** This post argued that software engineers should stop thinking of themselves as "programmers" and instead understand their role as revenue generators for businesses. Key points included:
  - Businesses hire engineers to solve business problems, not to write code
  - Understanding how your work connects to revenue is essential for career advancement
  - Salary is not determined by "fairness" but by market economics
  - Engineers systematically undervalue themselves
- **Impact:** This became one of the most widely-read and cited blog posts in the software industry. It fundamentally changed how many developers think about their careers and compensation. The post's influence extended far beyond McKenzie's core audience — it was shared widely in tech companies, bootcamps, and developer communities worldwide.

---

### January 23, 2012: "Salary Negotiation: Make More Money, Be More Valued"

- **Date:** January 23, 2012
- **Event:** Published "Salary Negotiation: Make More Money, Be More Valued"
- **Context:** A detailed, practical guide to salary negotiation for software engineers, covering topics like:
  - Why negotiation is expected and not rude
  - How to research market rates
  - Specific tactics for negotiation conversations
  - The psychology of compensation decisions
  - How companies actually determine salary bands
- **Impact:** This post became legendary in the tech community and is still widely recommended as essential reading for any software engineer. It has been credited with helping countless developers increase their compensation. The post exemplified McKenzie's core insight: understanding the economic system you operate in gives you leverage.

---

### 2012: "Charge More" Philosophy

- **Date:** ~2012
- **Event:** Published and popularized the "Charge More" philosophy
- **Context:** McKenzie consistently argued that software developers, freelancers, and small business owners systematically undercharge for their products and services. He wrote multiple posts about pricing strategy, including:
  - Why raising prices is often the highest-leverage move
  - How pricing signals quality
  - The economics of serving fewer customers at higher prices
  - Why "affordable" is not a competitive advantage
- **Impact:** "Charge More" became a rallying cry in the indie developer and bootstrapper community. McKenzie's pricing advice influenced how thousands of developers and small business owners thought about their products and services.

---

## 2013-2015: Indie Hacker Peak and Starfighter

### 2013-2014: Appointment Reminder Growth

- **Date:** 2013-2014
- **Event:** Continued building and growing Appointment Reminder
- **Context:** McKenzie wrote about the challenges of scaling a small SaaS business, including topics like:
  - Customer support at scale
  - Technical architecture for reliability
  - Pricing experiments and optimization
  - The realities of running a solo software business
- **Impact:** His transparent writing about the day-to-day realities of running a small software business provided invaluable education to aspiring indie hackers. He didn't just share successes — he shared failures, challenges, and the unglamorous aspects of the work.

---

### November 7, 2014: "Doing Business in Japan"

- **Date:** November 7, 2014
- **Event:** Published "Doing Business in Japan"
- **Context:** After nearly a decade of living and working in Japan, McKenzie wrote a comprehensive guide to Japanese business culture, covering topics like:
  - The role of consensus in decision-making
  - Business card etiquette and its deeper significance
  - How Japanese companies approach software development
  - The concept of "reading the air" (kuuki wo yomu)
  - Why Japan's tech industry operates differently from Silicon Valley
- **Impact:** This post became one of his most popular essays, offering insights that were both practically useful and culturally illuminating. It demonstrated his ability to analyze complex systems (in this case, a business culture) and extract actionable insights.

---

### ~2015: Starfighter

- **Date:** ~2015
- **Event:** Co-founded Starfighter (with Erin "Eevee" Ptacek)
- **Context:** Starfighter was a programming contest/game platform designed to serve as a recruiting tool for companies and a way for developers to showcase their skills. The idea was to create competitive programming challenges that would be more effective and fair than traditional tech hiring processes.
- **Impact:** Starfighter represented McKenzie's attempt to apply his understanding of software economics to the hiring market. While the venture ultimately did not succeed commercially (it was shut down around 2015-2016), it demonstrated his willingness to take risks and tackle systemic problems. The experience of building and shutting down Starfighter informed his later thinking about the challenges of disrupting established industries.

---

### ~2015: Starfighter Shutdown

- **Date:** ~2015-2016
- **Event:** Starfighter shut down
- **Context:** The venture faced challenges with monetization, market fit, and the general difficulty of disrupting the hiring industry. McKenzie was open about the experience of it not working out.
- **Impact:** The Starfighter experience reinforced lessons about market timing, the challenges of two-sided marketplaces, and the difference between a good idea and a viable business. McKenzie's transparency about failure added to his credibility and provided valuable lessons for other entrepreneurs.

---

## 2016: Joining Stripe and the Atlas Era

### April 2016: Stripe Atlas Announced

- **Date:** April 2016
- **Event:** Stripe Atlas announced publicly; McKenzie joins Stripe
- **Context:** Stripe Atlas is a service that helps entrepreneurs around the world incorporate a U.S. company, set up a bank account, and begin accepting payments. McKenzie joined Stripe to work on Atlas, bringing his deep knowledge of small business economics, international business, and the challenges faced by non-U.S. entrepreneurs.
- **Impact:** This was a pivotal career shift. McKenzie moved from running his own small businesses to working at one of the most important fintech companies in the world. His role at Stripe allowed him to apply his thinking about software economics at scale, helping thousands of entrepreneurs worldwide start and grow their businesses.

---

### 2016-2018: Working on Stripe Atlas

- **Date:** 2016-2018
- **Event:** Working on Stripe Atlas, helping international entrepreneurs incorporate in the U.S.
- **Context:** McKenzie's work on Atlas involved:
  - Understanding the regulatory landscape for international business incorporation
  - Navigating banking, payments, and compliance requirements
  - Building relationships with banks, legal partners, and government agencies
  - Writing and communicating about how financial infrastructure actually works
- **Impact:** This work deepened McKenzie's understanding of financial infrastructure — the "plumbing" of the financial system that most people never see. His writing about these topics became increasingly sophisticated and detailed, eventually leading to his newsletter "Bits about Money."

---

### 2016-2020: Continued Writing and Speaking

- **Date:** 2016-2020
- **Event:** Continued blogging at kalzumeus.com and speaking at conferences
- **Context:** Even while working at Stripe, McKenzie continued to write influential blog posts and speak at conferences like MicroConf and Business of Software. His topics expanded to include:
  - Financial infrastructure and payments
  - The economics of fintech
  - Regulatory systems and their impact on businesses
  - The mechanics of banking and compliance
- **Impact:** McKenzie's writing during this period bridged the gap between technical and business audiences, explaining complex financial systems in accessible terms. His ability to demystify financial infrastructure made him a unique voice in the tech community.

## 2020-2021: COVID, VaccinateCA, and Expanded Impact

### January 2021: VaccinateCA Founded

- **Date:** January 2021
- **Event:** Founded VaccinateCA
- **Context:** During the chaotic early COVID-19 vaccine rollout in the United States, McKenzie founded VaccinateCA — a volunteer-driven initiative to help people find available vaccine appointments. The approach was scrappy and effective: volunteers would literally call pharmacies and vaccination sites to check availability, then publish that data online and via phone lines.
- **Impact:** McKenzie described VaccinateCA as his most important work. The project demonstrated his ability to apply software thinking to real-world problems at scale, mobilizing volunteers and building systems to navigate a fragmented, confusing public health infrastructure. It was a departure from his commercial work but deeply aligned with his core skills: understanding complex systems, building practical solutions, and communicating clearly.

---

### 2021: VaccinateCA Impact

- **Date:** 2021
- **Event:** VaccinateCA helped thousands of people find vaccine appointments
- **Context:** The project operated during a period of extreme confusion and anxiety around vaccine availability. By providing reliable, real-time information, VaccinateCA helped people — particularly vulnerable populations — access vaccines they might otherwise have missed.
- **Impact:** The project earned McKenzie widespread recognition beyond the tech community and demonstrated the potential for software thinking to address public health challenges. It also reinforced his belief that understanding systems (in this case, healthcare logistics) and building practical tools could create enormous value.

### 2022-2023: Bits about Money Newsletter

- **Date:** ~2022-2023
- **Event:** Launched "Bits about Money" newsletter (bitsaboutmoney.com)
- **Context:** McKenzie started a newsletter focused on financial infrastructure — how payments, banking, and money movement actually work under the hood. Topics include:
  - Payment rails (ACH, wire transfers, card networks)
  - Banking-as-a-service and sponsor banks
  - Regulatory compliance (KYC/AML, money transmitter licensing)
  - The economics of fintech
  - How financial systems actually work vs. how people assume they work
- **Impact:** The newsletter became essential reading for anyone working in fintech or interested in how the financial system actually operates. McKenzie's ability to explain complex financial infrastructure in clear, engaging prose made the newsletter uniquely valuable.

- **Date:** ~2023-2024
- **Event:** Began hosting "Complex Systems" podcast
- **Context:** McKenzie started a weekly podcast exploring complex systems — financial, regulatory, technological, and social. The podcast allowed him to expand his communication style beyond written essays.
- **Impact:** The podcast format allowed McKenzie to explore topics in greater depth and with more nuance than blog posts, featuring conversations with experts across various domains.

### 2024-2025: Senate Banking Committee Testimony on Debanking

- **Date:** 2024-2025
- **Event:** Testified before the U.S. Senate Banking Committee on debanking
- **Context:** McKenzie testified about the practice of banks closing accounts or denying services to individuals and businesses, often without clear explanation or recourse. His testimony drew on his extensive experience in financial technology and his deep understanding of banking compliance systems.
- **Impact:** This represented a significant expansion of McKenzie's influence from the tech community into the political arena. His technical expertise and ability to explain complex systems made him an effective witness. He published a post titled "Bank CEO: Retract your debanking piece? Me: No." (February 10, 2025), demonstrating his willingness to stand by his analysis even under pressure.

## Evolution of Thinking — Key Themes by Era

### 2006-2010: The Builder Phase
- Focus: How to build and sell software products
- Key insight: Software engineers systematically undervalue themselves and their work
- Writing style: Detailed, data-driven, practical

### 2011-2015: The Career Strategist Phase
- Focus: How software engineers should think about their careers and compensation
- Key insight: Understanding business economics is more valuable than technical skill alone
- Writing style: Provocative, direct, sometimes contrarian

### 2016-2020: The Infrastructure Phase
- Focus: How financial systems actually work
- Key insight: The "plumbing" of the financial system is both more complex and more important than most people realize
- Writing style: Explanatory, demystifying, systems-oriented

### 2021-Present: The Public Intellectual Phase
- Focus: Applying systems thinking to public problems (healthcare, banking, regulation)
- Key insight: Understanding complex systems enables practical action at scale
- Writing style: Authoritative, politically engaged, broadening scope
