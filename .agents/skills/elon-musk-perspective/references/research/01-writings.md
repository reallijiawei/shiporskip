## 1. First-Hand Texts by Musk

### 1.1 "The Secret Tesla Motors Master Plan (just between you and me)" — August 2, 2006

**Source**: Tesla.com blog post (original URL: `https://www.tesla.com/blog/secret-tesla-motors-master-plan-just-between-you-and-me`; now returns 403 — content confirmed via Wikipedia's Tesla, Inc. article) ★★★★★

The post laid out a four-step strategy:

1. Build a sports car (Roadster) — high-price, low-volume, targeting early adopters "less sensitive to price"
2. Use that revenue to build an affordable car (Model S)
3. Use *that* revenue to build an even more affordable car (Model 3)
4. While doing the above, provide zero-emission electric power generation options

**Core insight**: Enter at the top of the market and work downward. Each stage funds the next and drives battery cost reductions through scale. This was Musk's deliberate counter to the conventional auto-industry approach of starting with mass-market vehicles.

**Wikipedia (Tesla, Inc.)** paraphrases: the strategy was to start with "high-price, low-volume vehicles" for customers "less sensitive to price," then leverage cost reductions to offer cheaper cars at scale. ★★★★

**Source**: Tesla.com blog post (original URL: `https://www.tesla.com/blog/master-plan-part-deux`; now returns 403) ★★★★★

## 2. Core Beliefs (Recurring 3+ Times Across Sources)

### 2.1 First Principles Thinking ★★★★★

Musk's most frequently cited intellectual framework. He has invoked this across dozens of interviews.

**TED Talk (2013)** with Chris Anderson:
> "I think it's important to reason from first principles rather than by analogy. So, you kind of boil things down to the most fundamental truths and say, 'OK, what are we sure is true?' and then reason up from there."

**Interview with Kevin Rose (2012)**: Musk demonstrated this by breaking down battery costs — rather than accepting market prices for battery packs, he calculated the raw material costs (cobalt, nickel, aluminum, etc.) and found they were much cheaper than assembled packs. This led to Tesla's in-house battery strategy.

**Attribution**: Musk has attributed this method to **Aristotle**, who defined first principles as "the first basis from which a thing is known."

**Wait But Why (Tim Urban, 2015)** describes this as reasoning like a "chef" (starting from scratch, reasoning from fundamentals) versus a "cook" (following recipes / convention).

Sources: Wait But Why (waitbutwhy.com/2015/05/elon-musk-the-worlds-raddest-man.html), Wikipedia (Tesla, Inc.), multiple interview references. ★★★★

## 3. Self-Coined Terms & Concepts

### 3.1 "First Principles Thinking" (his usage) ★★★★★

While the concept originates with Aristotle, Musk popularized its modern business application. His usage specifically means: strip away all inherited assumptions, identify the fundamental physical/economic constraints, and reason upward from those constraints to find solutions that conventional thinking misses.

**Key application**: Battery cost analysis — instead of accepting $600/kWh market price, Musk broke down raw material costs and found they were ~$80/kWh, leading to Tesla's in-house battery manufacturing strategy.
Source: Kevin Rose interview (2012), TED Talk (2013). ★★★★

---

### 3.2 "The Machine That Builds the Machine" ★★★★★

Musk's phrase for the idea that the **factory itself is the real product** — designing and optimizing the manufacturing process is as important (or more) than designing the end product.

**Quote**: "The machine that makes the machine is vastly harder than the machine itself."
Source: Multiple Tesla earnings calls and interviews. Referenced in Wikipedia (Tesla, Inc.) context of heavy automation investment. ★★★★

**Practical consequence**: Tesla invested heavily in gigacasting, rapid factory construction (Shanghai Gigafactory: first vehicle less than one year after groundbreaking), and vertical integration of supply chains.

**Over-automation lesson**: Heavy robotics investment during Model 3 production "slowed the production of the vehicles," leading Musk to acknowledge that "humans are underrated" in production — a correction to his initial over-automation bet.
Source: Wikipedia (Tesla, Inc., Tesla Model 3). ★★★★

---

### 3.3 "Step Function" Improvements ★★★★

Musk uses this term to describe non-incremental, discontinuous leaps in capability — as opposed to gradual improvement. A "step function" is a sudden jump from one level to a fundamentally higher one.

**Context**: Used in discussions of manufacturing throughput, battery cost reduction, and rocket reusability. The idea is that true innovation requires step-function changes, not marginal optimization.

Note: While widely attributed to Musk across Tesla earnings calls and interviews, I was unable to fetch a primary source with a direct quote during this research session. The term is well-established in Musk discourse but specific citation needs verification. ★★★

### 3.5 "The Algorithm" (Five-Step Manufacturing Process) ★★★★

Reportedly codified by former Tesla president Jon McNeill in his 2026 book *The Algorithm*. The five steps:

1. **Make requirements less dumb** — Question every requirement. Challenge assumptions.
2. **Delete the part or process** — If you're not adding back at least 10% of what you delete, you're not deleting enough.
3. **Simplify or optimize** — Only after steps 1 and 2. Don't optimize a thing that shouldn't exist.
4. **Accelerate cycle time** — Speed up every process, but only after simplification.
5. **Automate** — Automate last, not first. This is the reverse of what most companies do.

**Key insight**: The order matters. Most companies try to automate first, which Musk considers a mistake. You must question, delete, and simplify before accelerating or automating.

Source: Wikipedia (Elon Musk) references McNeill's 2026 book. Multiple secondary sources describe this framework. ★★★ (for the specific step wording; ★★★★ for the general framework being attributed to Musk)

1. **Science fiction as civilizational planning**: Foundation directly inspired SpaceX. Dune, Culture, and classic sci-fi shaped his thinking about humanity's future trajectory.
2. **AI safety cluster**: Bostrom, Russell, Barrat, Tegmark — a deliberate pattern of reading about existential risk from AI.

## 5. Decision-Making Framework (Synthesized from Sources)

### 5.1 Wait But Why's "Chef vs. Cook" Model ★★★★

Tim Urban's analysis (from the Musk profile series) describes Musk's thinking as that of a **chef** who reasons from scratch, versus a **cook** who follows recipes. The chef asks: "What are the fundamental constraints here? What does physics allow?" The cook asks: "What did the last person do?"

Source: waitbutwhy.com/2015/05/elon-musk-the-worlds-raddest-man.html

### 5.2 Impact-Maximizing Career Choice ★★★★

In college, Musk asked: **"What will most affect the future of humanity?"** His answer became a shortlist of five domains: the internet, sustainable energy, space exploration, AI, and rewriting human genetics. These became the blueprint for his life's work (Zip2, PayPal, Tesla, SpaceX, OpenAI/Neuralink/xAI).

Source: waitbutwhy.com/2015/05/elon-musk-the-worlds-raddest-man.html

### 5.3 "I Think Exactly What I Said" ★★★★

After press conferences or negotiations, when asked his "real angle," Musk reportedly said: "I think exactly what I said." He views advertising as manipulative and dishonest, refuses to run ads for Tesla, and detests vague claims like "studies say."

Source: waitbutwhy.com/2015/05/elon-musk-the-worlds-raddest-man.html

### 5.4 Humans as Hardware/Software ★★★★

Musk views people as having "hardware" (body/brain) and "software" (thinking patterns, values, habits). Learning is essentially downloading new data and algorithms. He found classroom learning painfully slow and preferred self-directed reading — reportedly 10 hours a day as a child.

Source: waitbutwhy.com/2015/05/elon-musk-the-worlds-raddest-man.html

### 5.5 Vertical Integration as Strategy ★★★★

Across Tesla and SpaceX, Musk favors controlling as much of the production process in-house as possible, rather than outsourcing. This applies to:
- Tesla: Batteries, motors, software, sales (direct-to-consumer, bypassing dealerships)
- SpaceX: Rocket engines, avionics, landing systems

"SpaceX could significantly cut launch costs" through vertical integration, off-the-shelf components, and modular software engineering.
Source: Wikipedia (SpaceX, Tesla, Inc.). ★★★★
